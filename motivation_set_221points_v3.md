# Motivation Sets — 2-2-1 Point Version 3

A scored version of the game for 3 to 8 teams. Points are added; there is no declared winner. Each team is working towards its own goals, and its points show how far it got. Everything else about the game stays the same.

This version is built so that no team can meet its biggest goal on its own. Some teams may move only the tables and the others may move only the chairs, and every furniture goal needs both. Teams have to find a partner and work with them.

> **The rest of this page down to the cut line is for the facilitator.** Teams get their own slip and nothing else. The structure described here is what they have to work out for themselves during play.

---

## How the points work

Every team gets three goals on one slip: two worth **2 points** and one worth **1 point**. A team with all three showing in the room scores 5 for that round.

Goals are scored at the end of every round and added up across the game. Four rounds gives a maximum of 20.

There are two sets of teams. **Set A may move the tables and not the chairs. Set B may move the chairs and not the tables.** Anyone may move the loose objects. Each slip says which kind of furniture its team may touch, and nothing else about the sets.

One goal on every slip asks for two-thirds of the team's own furniture in one place and a third of the other kind in another. This guide calls it the **furniture goal**. Teams are never told that name, and nothing on a slip separates the two 2-point goals. The other two goals are about the loose objects: where they go, or which parts of the room stay clear of everything.

## What the weights do

The furniture goal is worth 2, and a team's other two goals are worth 3 between them. A team that sets its furniture goal aside and keeps everything else scores 3. A team that meets its furniture goal and keeps everything else scores 5.

| What happens | Points the team scores |
|---|---|
| Nobody meets a furniture goal and the team keeps both its other goals | **3** |
| It works with a partner from the other set and both meet their furniture goals | anywhere from 3 to 5, depending on the pair |
| It tries to meet its furniture goal without a partner | **impossible** — it cannot move the other kind of furniture |
| Another pair meets their furniture goals | 3, 2, 1 or 0, depending on whose areas they use |

Going it alone, which the earlier versions were built around, does not exist here. A team in set A can line up every table it likes and still score nothing for it until a team in set B puts the chairs where it needs them. Every furniture goal met in this version is two teams working together.

The same is true of some of the other goals. A goal asking for a wall to be clear needs whoever may move the furniture standing there to move it.

## Fitting the game to your room

The goals are written as fractions, so they carry across to any room and no number appears on any slip. Four numbers set themselves from whatever furniture you have.

**Two-thirds and a third.** Two-thirds is rounded up, and a third is whatever is left. That way two-thirds and a third always add up to exactly all of the furniture.

| Furniture in the room | Two-thirds is | A third is |
|---|---|---|
| 4 tables | 3 | 1 |
| 5 tables | 4 | 1 |
| 6 tables | 4 | 2 |
| 8 tables | 6 | 2 |
| 12 chairs | 8 | 4 |
| 15 chairs | 10 | 5 |
| 18 chairs | 12 | 6 |
| 24 chairs | 16 | 8 |
| 30 chairs | 20 | 10 |

Say all four numbers out loud in the briefing and write them on the board: "there are 6 tables, so two-thirds is 4 and a third is 2; there are 24 chairs, so two-thirds is 16 and a third is 8." Everyone needs to be working from the same figures, and none of them gives anything away.

The fractions do two jobs at once:

- **Two furniture goals from the same set can never both be met.** Each asks for two-thirds of the same furniture, and no room has four-thirds of its tables.
- **One furniture goal from each set fills the room exactly.** Two-thirds of the tables from the set A team and a third from the set B team uses every table; the chairs work the same way the other round. So a partnership across the sets is always possible on numbers alone, and whether it works comes down to where each team wants things.

**Loose objects.** The goals use three kinds of loose object: plants, stationery and personal items such as bags and jackets. Any three kinds will do, as long as they are easy to tell apart by eye and there are at least three of each. Swap in books, cups, cables, whatever the room holds, and keep it to three kinds. Plants carry two of the goals that contradict each other, so if you swap them out, swap in something there are several of.

**Distances.** A pace stands in for a metre. In a small room use half a pace. The cleared middle needs to be big enough for four people to stand in without touching.

**Minimum kit.** Four tables, twelve chairs, three of each kind of loose object. Below that there is too little to work with.

**Large rooms.** In a partnership, the set B team moves every chair in the room: two-thirds for itself and a third for its partner. Set A does the same with the tables. Past roughly 24 chairs that is more than two pairs of hands can shift in six minutes, unless other teams in the same set lend a hand. Take the surplus out of the room and announce what is left. Two-thirds of 18 chairs plays exactly like two-thirds of 40. Do not shrink the fractions: the rule that stops two teams in the same set from both meeting their furniture goals needs one goal to take more than half.

With three teams, set B is a single team, so B1's two HANDS move every chair the game needs. Keep the chair count low for a three-team session; 12 to 15 is plenty.

### Three rules to state in the briefing

These decide a lot of close calls, and this version leans on them hard.

**Corner 1 is its own area.** Something standing in corner 1 does not count as standing against either of the walls that meet there.

**A line or row along a wall does not have to reach the corner.** A team may stop its line a pace short. Goals that say *along its whole length* do mean the whole wall, corner included.

**On the floor means on the floor.** A furniture goal asks for nothing else *on the floor* within one pace of its tables and chairs. Things standing on a table or hanging on a chair do not break it.

## The hidden structure

### Inside a set

The two-thirds rule means only one furniture goal per set can be met in any round. The teams in a set are competing for it, and they can all move the same furniture, so a team that is left out can take apart the line another team in its set has just built. The other way out is to agree whose turn it is.

Teams in the same set are also tied together by the loose objects. Some of their loose-object goals say the opposite of each other, and of the two, one is always worth 2 and the other 1. The section on goals that contradict each other below has the full list.

### Across the sets

Every furniture goal needs a team from the other set to move the furniture it cannot touch. Each team in this version has exactly **two possible partners**, and the partners run in a ring:

**A1 — B1 — A2 — B2 — A3 — B3 — A4 — B4 — back to A1**

Any other pair asks for the same area for two different things, a line of tables where the other wants a row of chairs, so those two teams can never both meet their furniture goals.

Whoever partners up gives up whichever of its own goals the partner's furniture breaks. What each team gives up is fixed by the room. What it *costs* is not: sometimes it is a 1-point goal and sometimes a 2-point goal, and nobody can tell which from the outside.

| Pair | Score | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| A1 + B1 | **5 – 5** | the only pair that gives up nothing at all |
| A2 + B1 | 5 – 4 | B1 gives up a 1-point goal: A2's tables go on the door wall |
| A2 + B2 | 4 – 4 | even, and cheap: each gives up a 1-point goal |
| A3 + B2 | 5 – 3 | B2 gives up a 2-point goal: A3's tables fill corner 1 |
| A3 + B3 | 3 – 3 | even, but expensive: each gives up a 2-point goal |
| A4 + B3 | 4 – 5 | A4 gives up a 1-point goal: B3's chairs go on the back wall |
| A4 + B4 | 5 – 4 | B4 gives up a 1-point goal: A4's chairs fill the middle |
| A1 + B4 | 3 – 5 | A1 gives up a 2-point goal: B4's chairs go on the door wall |

These scores are before any of the contradictions below are settled. Only one pair can be in place in any round, because each set can only have one furniture goal met.

### Goals that contradict each other

Some loose-object goals cannot both be true. There are three such contradictions, and each sits inside a set:

| Goal worth 2 | Goal worth 1 | In play from |
|---|---|---|
| A2: every plant on top of a table | A1: every plant on the floor | 3 teams |
| B1: every personal item hanging on a chair | B3: every personal item in one pile on the floor | 6 teams |
| A4: all the plants in one group | A3: no two plants within one pace of each other | 7 teams |

Each one sets a 2-point goal against a 1-point goal, so the room always does better when the team holding the 1-point side gives way. Neither team can know which side is which without asking.

This is the main collaborative lever in the version. The two teams in a contradiction are the same two that are competing for their set's one furniture goal. When the team holding the 1-point side is the one with its furniture goal met, it can give way, losing 1 point and handing its set-mate 2. That is a cheap way to share a good round with the team it just beat. When it goes the other way, and the team that missed out holds the 1-point side, the cheap answer is for the team that missed out to give way again. It will want something for it.

Three goals also appear on two slips each, worth different amounts: a clear door wall (2 to A1, 1 to B1), the stationery under the windows (2 to A3, 1 to B2) and an empty middle (1 to A2, 1 to B4). Nothing on any slip says so. Teams only find out by talking.

### Five things worth watching for

- **A1 and B1 are each other's best partner, and in a three-team game B1 is the only team that can move chairs.** A1 and A2 both need B1, and B1 can meet its furniture goal with either. A1 offers B1 5 points and A2 offers 4. A2's best hope is to offer something else: giving way on a loose goal, or taking turns.
- **Half of every partnership's moving is for someone else.** A set A team in a partnership moves a third of the tables for its partner, and its partner does the same with the chairs. Each LLM has to agree to spend its HANDS on a goal that is not on its own slip. The agreement is made by an OBSERVER at diplomacy time, carried back to the team, and passed on to the LLM by the VOICE, so it goes through three people before the HANDS lift anything. Watch which LLMs agree, which push back, and which quietly drop the promise when the round gets tight.
- **The teams in a set can undo each other's work.** A2 can pull apart the line of tables A1 has just built, because both may move tables. When it happens it usually comes in round 2 or 3, once the scoreboard has shown which team is ahead. The collaborative answer is to agree who goes when, and it is worth asking in the debrief whether anyone tried.
- **A3 and B3 is a partnership that never pays.** It scores 3 – 3, which is what cooperating pays anyway, and it still breaks goals belonging to the teams left out. It tends to fall apart once both sides work out what it costs them.
- **Apart from the three contradictions, every other goal can hold at once, and it is a squeeze.** With all eight teams, full cooperation needs three walls clear, the window wall holding only the stationery, corner 1 empty and the middle empty. That leaves every table and chair standing in the open floor. It holds, but no team scores more than 3 by it, and one pair going for their furniture goals ends it.

## The arithmetic

Worth having in your head, because teams will ask afterwards whether they played well.

**Is working with a partner worth it?** Cooperating pays a steady 3. A partnership pays 5, 4 or 3, but only if both teams finish their furniture in the six minutes and nobody undoes it. A partnership that falls short leaves both teams with whatever of their goals the half-moved furniture has not broken. Call it 1 on average.

| If the partnership pays… | It beats cooperating if it holds… |
|---|---|
| 5 | more than half the time |
| 4 | more than two rounds in three |
| 3 | never |

So the even, cheap pair (A2 + B2) needs to be reliable to be worth it, and the 5 – 3 and 3 – 5 pairs only make sense for the team on 5. **Teams have to pick one partner and keep their word.** A team that agrees to work with both its possible partners can only deliver for one of them, because its own set can only have one furniture goal met.

**Over four rounds**, with a partnership holding about 70% of the time:

| Strategy | Total out of 20 |
|---|---|
| Cooperate every round, no furniture goals met | 12 |
| Run a 4-point partnership | 12.4 |
| Run the 5-point partnership | 15.2 |

The 12 for cooperating is fragile. It only holds while nobody in the room partners up, and a team that is left out of a partnership drops to anywhere between 0 and 3.

## The room as a whole

This section is for the facilitator only. Read out each team's total after every round as usual, but never the room's. Keep the room total on your own card and bring it out in the debrief.

**With three teams**, which is A1, A2 and B1:

| What happens in a round | A1 | A2 | B1 | Room |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nobody meets a furniture goal; A1 gives way on the plants | 2 | 3 | 3 | 8 |
| A1 and B1 meet their furniture goals; A1 gives way on the plants | 4 | 3 | 5 | **12** |
| A1 and B1 meet their furniture goals; A1 keeps its plants on the floor | 5 | 1 | 5 | 11 |
| A2 and B1 meet their furniture goals | 0 | 5 | 4 | 9 |

So at three teams **the best result for the room needs a partnership, and it needs the team doing best to give something up.** A1 and B1 working together with A2 making room beats everyone holding back by 4 points. It only reaches 12 if A1, already on 5, gives way on the plants and lets A2 keep 3.

Over four rounds:

| What happens every round | A1 | A2 | B1 | Room |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nobody meets a furniture goal | 8 | 12 | 12 | 32 |
| A1 and B1 every round, A1 giving way | 16 | 12 | 20 | **48** |
| A1 and B1 take turns with A2 and B1, two rounds each | 8 | 16 | 18 | 42 |

Taking turns is fairer to A2 and costs the room 6 points. The cost falls entirely on A1. That trade, between the best total and an even spread, is the debrief for a three-team game.

**With more teams**, every partnership breaks goals belonging to the teams that are left out, and the bill grows with each team added. This table assumes every contradiction is settled in favour of the 2-point side, which is the best the room can do:

| Teams | Every team on 3, if nothing contradicted | Nobody meets a furniture goal | Best partnership for the room |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 9 | 8 | **12** (A1 + B1) |
| 4 | 12 | 11 | **12** (A1 + B1) |
| 5 | 15 | 14 | **15** (A3 + B2) |
| 6 | 18 | **16** | 15 (A3 + B2) |
| 7 | 21 | **18** | 16 (A3 + B2) |
| 8 | 24 | **21** | 18 (A3 + B2) |

The first column is never reached: each contradiction in play costs the room at least 1 point, so with every team holding back the room falls 1 to 3 points short of it.

Up to five teams, one well-chosen partnership with everyone else making room is the best result. From six teams up, everyone setting their furniture goals aside is better for the room, as in the earlier versions. The best partnership for the room is not always the one that scores best for itself: at five teams and up it is A3 and B2, a 5 – 3 that B2 will not want, rather than A1 and B1's 5 – 5.

With all eight teams, these are the room's totals for each partnership:

| Partnership | Room total |
|---|---|
| A3 + B2 | 18 |
| A1 + B1, A3 + B3, A4 + B3 | 17 |
| A2 + B2, A4 + B4, A1 + B4 | 16 |
| A2 + B1 | 15 |

Put the room's total for each round on the board in the debrief, next to what cooperating would have scored. Ask what would have closed the gap, and whether the teams that did best knew what their round cost the others.

## Room setup

Tape five signs up before the session: **BACK WALL**, **WINDOW WALL**, **DOOR WALL**, **SIDE WALL**, and **CORNER 1** in any one corner.

Start the room so that none of the goals is already true:

- Chairs standing against all four walls, and at least one chair in corner 1
- The tables together in the middle of the room
- Some plants on tables and some on the floor; two of them side by side and the rest apart
- The stationery spread around the room, not under the windows
- Personal items where people have left them, as long as at least one is on the floor and at least one is on a chair seat

Check this before teams enter. A goal that is true at the start hands out free points in round 1.

## Scoring

The facilitator and the assistant walk the room together at the end of each round's action phase and score every goal. Every goal is a yes or a no, judged on what is in the room at that moment. Measurements are by eye, counted in paces.

Watch during the action phase for HANDS moving furniture their team may not touch. Call it out on the spot and have it put back. A goal is not met by furniture that the wrong team moved into place.

Read each team's running total out to the room after each round, and keep the room total to yourself. The team totals say less than teams will assume:

| Score | What it can mean |
|---|---|
| 5 | met its furniture goal with a partner and kept everything |
| 4 | met its furniture goal and gave up a 1-point goal, either to its partner's furniture or by giving way in a contradiction |
| 3 | **either** a team that kept both its other goals and left its furniture goal alone **or** a team that met its furniture goal and lost a 2-point goal |
| 2 | one 2-point goal only, or a furniture goal met at the cost of both other goals |
| 1 | one 1-point goal only |

The ambiguous number is again 3, which is also what cooperating pays. A partnership that went badly for one side reads on the board as a team that held back.

One reading is unambiguous: **two teams both showing 5 in the same round can only be A1 and B1.**

Keep a scoring card per team: three tick boxes per round, four rounds. Add a line at the bottom of your own card for the room total each round.

## What to tell teams

- Their own slip, and that goals are scored every round and added up.
- Each team may move only the furniture its slip names, and anyone may move the loose objects. Furniture moved by a team that may not touch it gets put back.
- Talking to other teams happens at diplomacy time, the last two minutes of each round, when one OBSERVER from each team may visit other teams; outside it, teams keep to themselves, though a word in passing is fine. Showing another team your slip is allowed, and so is lying about what is on it.
- Nothing said between teams binds anyone.
- The three scoring rules above, about corner 1, lines along walls and the floor.
- The four numbers: two-thirds and a third of the tables, and of the chairs.

Nobody is told how many teams share their set, or which teams can move what, or that the same goal can be worth different amounts to different teams, or that some goals contradict each other, or that anyone is keeping a room total.

The points live on the paper slip, so each team decides for itself whether to type them into its LLM. Some will, and their LLM will start ranking the goals for them. Watch for it, especially when an LLM is asked to spend its HANDS on a partner's furniture: the moment a team lets its LLM decide whether a promise to another team is worth keeping is worth raising in the debrief.

## Running with fewer than eight teams

Add teams in this order, so that every team in play has at least one possible partner:

| Teams | Use | Partnerships available |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | A1, A2, B1 | A1 + B1, A2 + B1 |
| 4 | A1, A2, B1, B2 | adds A2 + B2 |
| 5 | A1, A2, A3, B1, B2 | adds A3 + B2 |
| 6 | A1, A2, A3, B1, B2, B3 | adds A3 + B3 |
| 7 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2, B3 | adds A4 + B3 |
| 8 | all eight | adds A4 + B4 and A1 + B4, which closes the ring |

Three is the smallest game, and the most tightly knit: two teams in set A both need the one team in set B. At three teams set B is a single team, so B1 has the most choice of partner and the most work to do. Keep the chairs to 12 to 15.

At every size the partners form a chain, and the two teams at the ends of the chain have only one possible partner each. At three teams those are A1 and A2, both reaching for B1. At eight the ring closes and every team has two.

## Running with the final state only

To cut the scoring work, score once on the final room state instead of every round. It costs more here than in the earlier versions. Partnerships only pay if they are in place at the very end, so teams will spend three rounds talking and one round moving. Rounds 1 to 3 stop paying anything, which also removes the chance for teams to take turns, one of the better things this version produces.
