# Motivation Sets — 2-2-1 Point Version 4

A scored version of the game for three teams. Points are added; there is no declared winner. Each team is working towards its own goals, and its points show how far it got. Everything else about the game stays the same.

Anyone may move anything in the room. What keeps the three teams apart is not where things go but how they are arranged: which way the chairs face, what stands on top of a table and what goes underneath it. Any two teams can work together and both do well. The third team is always left with less.

> **The rest of this page down to the cut line is for the facilitator.** Teams get their own slip and nothing else. The structure described here is what they have to work out for themselves during play.

---

## How the points work

Every team gets three goals on one slip: two worth **2 points** and one worth **1 point**. A team with all three showing in the room scores 5 for that round.

Goals are scored at the end of every round and added up across the game. Four rounds gives a maximum of 20.

Every slip has the same three kinds of goal, though nothing on a slip says so:

- **The chair goal** (2 points) asks for two-thirds of the chairs set out a particular way.
- **The table goal** (2 points) asks for the tables and the loose objects to be arranged a particular way: what stands on top, what goes underneath, and where those tables stand.
- **The 1-point goal** is about the chairs again. It describes the way this team would most like its own chair goal done.

## What the weights do

| What happens | Points the team scores |
|---|---|
| It works with one other team and both meet their chair goals | **4** — both 2-point goals, never the 1-point goal |
| Two other teams work together, and leave room for its table goal | **3** |
| Two other teams work together, and do not | **1** |
| It goes it alone, meets its chair goal its own way and keeps everything | **5** |
| It goes it alone and the attempt is broken | 0, 1 or 2 |
| Nobody meets a chair goal, and every team keeps its table goal | 3 |

A partnership is worth 4 to each partner, and the team left out can get 3 at most. Whether it gets 3 or 1 is mostly up to the partners.

Going it alone is the only way to score 5, and the hardest thing in the game to hold. The section on going it alone below explains why.

## Fitting the game to your room

The goals are written as fractions, so they carry across to any room and no number appears on any slip. One number sets itself from whatever furniture you have.

**Two-thirds of the chairs.** Two-thirds is rounded up.

| Chairs in the room | Two-thirds is |
|---|---|
| 9 | 6 |
| 10 | 7 |
| 12 | 8 |
| 14 | 10 |
| 15 | 10 |
| 18 | 12 |

Say the number out loud in the briefing and write it on the board: "there are 15 chairs, so two-thirds is 10." Everyone needs to be working from the same figure, and it gives nothing away. Rounding up matters: it is what stops all three chair goals from ever being met at once.

**Tables.** Any number from two upwards works. Four to six is best. Every table needs something on it or under it for one of the goals, so have at least as many loose objects in the room as there are tables.

**Loose objects.** The goals use three kinds: plants, stationery and personal items such as bags and jackets. Any three kinds will do, as long as they are easy to tell apart by eye. Have at least two of each kind. If you swap one out, swap in something a team could plausibly want on a table or under it.

**Walls.** Number the walls 1 to 4 in order round the room, so that **WALL 1 and WALL 3 face each other**. The chair goals depend on that, and on nothing else about the room.

**Minimum kit.** Nine chairs, two tables, two of each kind of loose object. Below that there is too little to work with.

**Large rooms.** A team going it alone moves two-thirds of the chairs with two pairs of hands. Past about 18 chairs that is more than six minutes allows, and going it alone starts failing on the clock alone. Take the surplus out of the room and announce what is left. Two-thirds of 15 chairs plays exactly like two-thirds of 30.

### Rules to state in the briefing

These decide a lot of close calls, and this version leans on them hard.

**Against a wall** means touching it, or as close as the chair or table will go.

**A chair faces** whichever way the front of its seat points. **Its back is against a wall** when the back of the chair touches the wall.

**A goal about two-thirds of the chairs is met if any two-thirds of them meet it.** What the other chairs are doing does not matter to that goal.

**Underneath a table** means on the floor under its top. A chair tucked under a table counts as something underneath it.

**A row along a wall does not have to reach the corner.**

## The hidden structure

### The chairs: any two fit, never all three

| Team | Two-thirds of the chairs… |
|---|---|
| A | in one row **against** WALL 1, all facing the same way |
| B | **facing** WALL 1 |
| C | with their **backs against** a wall |

Any two of these can hold at once:

- **A and B:** a row against WALL 1, every chair facing the wall.
- **A and C:** a row against WALL 1, every chair with its back to the wall, facing into the room.
- **B and C:** a row against WALL 3 with their backs to it, facing across the room towards WALL 1.

All three never can. A chair standing against WALL 1 and facing it has nothing behind it, and a chair with its back against WALL 1 is facing away from it. A's "all facing the same way" closes the last gap: without it, a room with every team working together could split the chairs three ways and meet all three goals.

So in any round at most two chair goals are met, and whichever two teams meet theirs, the third cannot. A and B both name WALL 1, so they look like natural partners. C's "a wall" looks like it fits with anything. None of that tells a team which partnerships actually work: all three do.

### The 1-point goal: each team's own way

Each team's 1-point goal describes how it would set out its own chair goal if nobody else had a say. Each possible partner wants it done a different way.

| Team | Its own way | With the first partner | With the other partner |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Chairs against WALL 1 stand side-on, facing along the wall | B turns them to face the wall | C turns their backs to the wall |
| B | Chairs facing WALL 1 stand in the open floor, away from every wall | A pushes them against WALL 1 | C backs them against WALL 3 |
| C | No chair with its back against WALL 1 or WALL 3 (so its row goes on WALL 2 or WALL 4) | A backs them against WALL 1 | B backs them against WALL 3 |

So **a team that works with a partner always loses its 1-point goal**, whichever partner it picks. A team that is left out keeps its 1-point goal, because no partnership touches it. A team's own chair goal never breaks its own 1-point goal: done its own way, the two hold together.

In diplomacy, this shows up as an argument about how the chairs should face. Both partners have a reason to want it their own way, and neither can have it.

### The tables and loose objects: all three fit, but only with care

| Team | Table goal |
|---|---|
| A | Every plant and every piece of stationery on top of a table, and those tables pushed together into one block |
| B | Every table used for one thing only: something on top or something underneath, never both, and no table left empty |
| C | Every personal item on the floor underneath a table, and every table with a personal item under it standing against a wall |

These look as if they clash. "Everything on top" against "bags underneath" against "never both" reads like three teams pulling the same tables three ways. In fact all three can hold at once: a block of tables with the plants and stationery on top and nothing underneath, and the other tables against a wall with the personal items under them and nothing on top.

That arrangement does not happen by accident. Each partnership, left to itself, tends to break the third team's table goal without noticing:

- **A and B** spread the plants and stationery over every table, to make sure none is left empty. That leaves no clear table for C's personal items.
- **A and C** put the personal items under the same tables that hold the plants. That breaks B's never-both rule.
- **B and C** scatter the personal items under every table. That leaves no clear tops for A's block.

Leaving room costs the partners nothing but a little care, and it is worth 2 points to the team left out. The left-out team can try to fix it, but that means rearranging tables and objects the partners have just set out, with two pairs of hands, in six minutes. One object put back in the wrong place undoes it.

One goal pulls in the left-out team's favour. B's "no table left empty" is easiest to meet when every table has a use. When A and B work together, the spare tables are exactly where C's personal items belong. Helping C also helps B.

The chairs and tables meet in one place: a chair tucked under a table counts as something underneath it. Spare chairs can fill B's empty tables. A chair pushed under a table that has things on top breaks B's goal.

### Going it alone

A team that meets its chair goal its own way keeps its 1-point goal, and if its table goal holds too it scores 5. Nothing else in the game pays that. It is also the hardest result to hold:

- **The work.** Two-thirds of the chairs and the whole table goal, with two pairs of hands, while two other teams are moving things too.
- **One object undoes it.** Turning one chair in A's row breaks both A's chair goal ("all facing the same way") and A's 1-point goal. Pushing one of B's chairs against a wall breaks B's 1-point goal. Backing one chair against WALL 1 or WALL 3 breaks C's. Every table goal can be broken by moving one object.
- **Both other teams have a reason to stop it.** A team going it alone leaves the other two on 3 at best. If those two work together instead, they get 4 each, and they have four pairs of hands against two.

When it holds, going it alone costs the room nothing: 5, 3 and 3 is the same 11 as a partnership that leaves room. That is worth raising in the debrief.

### Things worth watching for

- **Who partners with whom, and how quickly.** Every partnership works, so the question is only who gets there first. The team that waits is the one left out.
- **Whether the partners leave room for the third team's table goal.** It is the cheapest favour in the game. Watch whether anyone thinks of it without being asked, and whether the left-out team knows to ask.
- **The argument about which way the chairs face.** Each partner's 1-point goal wants the chairs a different way, and both must lose it. Watch which LLMs accept the compromise and which keep steering the HANDS back towards their own way.
- **Who is left out twice.** Three teams and four rounds do not divide. Somebody sits out two rounds, and the round-4 diplomacy is often about who that should be.
- **Spoiling.** The left-out team can turn one chair or move one plant and cost a partner 2 points. It costs nothing to do. Watch whether it is threatened, used, or traded for a promise about the next round.

## The arithmetic

Worth having in your head, because teams will ask afterwards whether they played well.

**Is working together worth it?** A partnership pays 4 a round, and being left out pays 3 or 1. Nobody does better by holding back: if no chair goal is met, each team scores 3 at best, and that only if all three table goals hold, which takes a lot of work.

**Is going it alone worth it?** It pays 5 if it holds. An attempt that is broken leaves the team with 0 to 2, call it 1. So going it alone beats a partnership only if it holds more than three rounds in four. Against two teams with every reason to stop it, it rarely does.

**Over four rounds:**

| What happens | A | B | C | Room |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A and B every round, leaving room for C | 16 | 16 | 12 | **44** |
| A and B every round, leaving C no room | 16 | 16 | 4 | 36 |
| Taking turns (A+B, B+C, A+C, A+B), leaving room | 15 | 15 | 14 | **44** |
| Nobody meets a chair goal, all table goals hold | 12 | 12 | 12 | 36 |

## The room as a whole

This section is for the facilitator only. Read out each team's total after every round as usual, but never the room's. Keep the room total on your own card and bring it out in the debrief.

| What happens in a round | Scores | Room |
|---|---|---|
| A partnership that leaves room for the third team's table goal | 4 / 4 / 3 | **11** |
| A team going it alone, everyone else keeping their table goals | 5 / 3 / 3 | **11** |
| A partnership that leaves no room | 4 / 4 / 1 | 9 |
| Nobody meets a chair goal, all table goals hold | 3 / 3 / 3 | 9 |
| Two sides fighting over the chairs, nothing finished | 1 or 2 each | 3 to 6 |

The most the room can score in a round is **11, or 44 over four rounds**. A chair goal has to be met to get there, which means somebody is always on 3 or less. The best the room can do is never even.

Over the game, the best total and the fairest spread can go together: taking turns as partners, and always leaving room, still reaches 44. But someone is left out twice, and whoever that is finishes behind. Put the room's total for each round on the board in the debrief next to 11. Ask what closed or opened the gap, and who decided which team would be left out.

## Room setup

Tape four signs up before the session: **WALL 1**, **WALL 2**, **WALL 3** and **WALL 4**, in order round the room, so that WALL 1 and WALL 3 face each other.

Start the room so that none of the goals is already true:

- At least one chair against WALL 1, facing it
- At least one chair with its back against WALL 3
- The other chairs scattered, facing all different ways, with no more than a third facing WALL 1 and no more than a third backed against walls
- At least one table with something on top and something underneath, and at least one table left empty
- At least one plant on the floor
- At least one personal item on the floor in the open, away from every table

Check this before teams enter. A goal that is true at the start hands out free points in round 1.

## Scoring

The facilitator and the assistant walk the room together at the end of each round's action phase and score all nine goals. Every goal is a yes or a no, judged on what is in the room at that moment, by eye.

Read each team's running total out to the room after each round, and keep the room total to yourself. The team totals say less than teams will assume:

| Score | What it can mean |
|---|---|
| 5 | went it alone and everything held |
| 4 | met its chair goal and its table goal — almost always a partnership |
| 3 | **either** left out with room made for its table goal **or** nobody met a chair goal **or** went it alone without its table goal |
| 2 | one 2-point goal only |
| 1 | only its 1-point goal — usually left out with no room made |

Two teams both on 4 in the same round are partners. The third team's score then shows whether they left it room.

Keep a scoring card per team: three tick boxes per round, four rounds. Add a line at the bottom of your own card for the room total each round.

## What to tell teams

- Their own slip, and that goals are scored every round and added up.
- Anyone may move anything in the room.
- Talking to other teams happens at diplomacy time, the first two minutes of each round, when one OBSERVER from each team may visit other teams; outside it, teams keep to themselves, though a word in passing is fine. Showing another team your slip is allowed, and so is lying about what is on it.
- Nothing said between teams binds anyone.
- The judging rules above.
- The number: what two-thirds of the chairs comes to.

Nobody is told that every slip has a chair goal, a table goal and a 1-point goal, that any two teams can work together, or that anyone is keeping a room total.

The points live on the paper slip, so each team decides for itself whether to type them into its LLM. Some will, and their LLM will start ranking the goals for them. Watch for it, especially when the partners argue over which way the chairs face: the moment a team lets its LLM decide whether to give up its own way is worth raising in the debrief.

## Running the game with more people

This version is built for exactly three teams. With more people, give each team a third OBSERVER, or run two rooms side by side, each with its own three slips.

## Running with the final state only

To cut the scoring work, score once on the final room state instead of every round. It costs a lot here. Taking turns as partners stops meaning anything, and so does being left out twice, which is what most of the debrief is about. Rounds 1 to 3 become pure negotiation.
