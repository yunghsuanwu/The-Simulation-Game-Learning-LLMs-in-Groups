# Motivation Sets — Point Version, 2-2-1

A scored version of the game for 8 teams. It replaces the "no declared winner" rule; everything else about the game stays the same.

> **The rest of this page down to the cut line is for the facilitator.** Teams get their own slip and nothing else. The structure described here is what they have to work out for themselves during play.

---

## How the points work

Every team gets three goals on one slip: two worth **2 points** and one worth **1 point**. A team with all three showing in the room scores 5 for that round.

Goals are scored at the end of every round and added up across the game. Four rounds gives a maximum of 20.

There are two sets of four teams. **Set A moves the tables. Set B moves the chairs.** One goal on every slip claims two areas of the room and demands sole use of them. This guide calls it the **betrayal goal**, because the team that takes it does so at the expense of the other three teams that want the same furniture. Teams are never told that name, and nothing on a slip separates the two 2-point goals. The other two goals ask for one area to be cleared or filled with small objects.

## What the weights do

The betrayal goal is worth 2, and a team's other two goals are worth 3 between them. So a team that drops its betrayal goal and holds everything else scores more than a team that takes it and loses the rest. That single fact shapes the whole game:

| What happens | Points |
|---|---|
| Everyone in a set cooperates | **3 each** |
| One team betrays its set and it works | 5 for the betraying team |
| A betrayal that costs you both other goals | **2 — less than cooperating** |
| One betrayal, counted across the whole set | **4 lost** |
| Collusion across the sets | 4, sometimes 3, and once 5 |

Cooperating pays 60% of the maximum, so a team has a lot to lose by gambling. Betrayal is also hard to execute, because it is only worth attempting if you can hold your other two goals at the same time — which splits two pairs of hands between taking ground and defending it.

The same soft goal is worth 2 points to one team and 1 point to another. Nothing on any slip says so. Teams find out only by talking, and it is the single most useful thing they can learn about each other.

## Fitting the game to your room

The goals are written as fractions, so they carry across to any room and no number appears on any slip. Two numbers set themselves from whatever furniture you have.

**Tables.** Every set A betrayal goal asks for *half the tables* in one place and *the other half* in another, so it always uses every table in the room. That is what stops two teams in set A from both succeeding, whatever the count. An odd table goes in either group. Four tables or more works well; below that the lines are too short to read as anything.

**Chairs.** Every set B betrayal goal asks for *a third of the chairs* in one place and *another third* in another. Two teams doing that at once would need four thirds of the chairs, which no room has, so again only one of them can succeed. Round a third upwards.

| Chairs in the room | A third is | One betrayal goal needs | Two teams would need |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
| 15 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
| 18 | 6 | 12 | 24 |
| 20 | 7 | 14 | 28 |
| 24 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
| 30 | 10 | 20 | 40 |
| 36 | 12 | 24 | 48 |
| 40 | 14 | 28 | 56 |

Say both numbers out loud in the briefing and write them on the board: "there are 24 chairs, so a third is 8; there are 6 tables, so half is 3." Everyone needs to be working from the same figures, and neither figure gives anything away.

**Loose objects.** Three of the goals each claim a different kind of loose object: plants, stationery, personal items. Any three kinds will do, as long as they are easy to tell apart by eye and there are several of each. Swap in bags, books, cups, cables, whatever the room holds. Keep it to three distinct kinds.

**Distances.** A pace stands in for a metre. In a small room use half a pace. The cleared middle needs to be big enough for four people to stand in without touching.

**Minimum kit.** Four tables, twelve chairs, three kinds of loose object. Below that there is too little to fight over.

**Large rooms.** A fraction holds its difficulty steady against the room, so the work grows as the room does. Past roughly 36 chairs or 8 tables, a betrayal goal asks for more furniture than two pairs of hands can shift in six minutes, and it starts failing on the clock alone rather than on anything a team decided.

Fix that by taking furniture out of play and leaving the fractions alone. Move the surplus out of the room, then announce what is left: a third of 24 chairs plays exactly like a third of 60. Shrinking the fraction instead breaks the game, because the rule that stops two teams in a set from both winning needs the fraction to stay above a quarter, and a quarter exactly fails at every count that divides by four.

### Two scoring rules to state in the briefing

These decide a lot of close calls, and this version leans on them hard.

**Corner 1 is its own area.** Something standing in corner 1 does not count as standing against either of the walls that meet there. So a pile of plants in corner 1 does not break a goal asking for a clear back wall.

**A line or row along a wall does not have to reach the corner.** A team may stop its line a pace short, and a goal asking for nothing within one pace of that line is judged against the line as laid, not against the whole wall. Goals that say *along its whole length* do mean the whole wall, corner included.

## The hidden structure

### Inside a set

The four betrayal goals in a set want the same furniture in four different places, so only one team in a set can have its own. Each team's other two goals survive its own betrayal goal and are broken by its rivals'.

That produces two outcomes:

- **All four teams drop their betrayal goal and help each other.** Every team gets both its other goals. **3 points each, 12 for the set.**
- **One team pushes its betrayal goal through and the others give way.** **5 points for that team.** Whichever team betrays, the other three always land on 0, 1 and 2 in some order. **8 for the set.**

So a successful betrayal moves 4 points out of the set and hands 2 of them to the betraying team, which gains 2 while the set loses 4, and it shows up on the scoreboard the same evening.

### Across the sets

Set A claims the back wall, the window wall, corner 1 and the middle. Set B claims the door wall, the side wall, corner 1 and the middle. Because the two sets meet only in corner 1 and the middle, a team from A and a team from B can often hold their betrayal goals at the same time, each giving up whichever of its own goals the other's betrayal goal destroys. That is collusion.

What each team gives up is fixed by the geometry. What it *costs* is not — for four teams the goal that gets destroyed is their 1-pointer, and for four it is their 2-pointer. Collusion is cheap for some teams and expensive for others, and nobody can tell which from the outside.

This is the full table of pairs that can both hold their betrayal goals:

| Pair | Score | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| A1 + B1 | **5 – 5** | the only pair that gives up nothing at all |
| A4 + B2 | 4 – 4 | even, and cheap: each gives up a 1-point goal |
| A2 + B4 | 3 – 3 | even, but expensive: each gives up a 2-point goal |
| A2 + B1 | 5 – 4 | A2 gives up nothing |
| A1 + B4 | 4 – 5 | B4 gives up nothing |
| A1 + B2 | 3 – 5 | B2 gives up nothing; A1 loses its 2-point goal |
| A4 + B1 | 5 – 3 | A4 gives up nothing; B1 loses its 2-point goal |
| A3 + B1 | 5 – 2 | A3 gives up nothing; B1 loses both |
| A1 + B3 | 2 – 5 | B3 gives up nothing; A1 loses both |

Every other pair (A2+B2, A2+B3, A3+B2, A3+B3, A3+B4, A4+B3, A4+B4) wants the same corner or the same middle, so those two teams can never both hold their betrayal goals.

Only one team per set can take its betrayal goal at a time, so **at most one of these pairs can be live in any round.** Being the team in your set that gets the collusion is the thing worth competing for.

### Five things worth watching for

- **A1 and B1 are each other's perfect partner, and neither can know it without showing its slip.** A1's betrayal goal breaks nothing set B wants; B1's betrayal goal breaks nothing set A wants; and their four remaining goals all hold together. It is the only 5 – 5 in the game. But A1 has four possible partners and scores 5, 4, 3 and 2 across them, and B1 has the same spread. Both will spend the game talking, and both will end up badly if they find each other last. This is the one place where showing another team the real slip pays better than lying about it.
- **The pair that scores best does the least damage to the room.** A1 and B1 both taking their betrayal goals leaves 16 points in the room. A4 and B2 both taking theirs, for 4 each, leaves 10. The two teams doing best are not the two doing the most harm, which is worth putting to the group directly in the debrief.
- **A3 and B3 have no collusion available in practice.** A3's only compatible partner is B1, on terms of 5 – 2 in A3's favour, and B1 will never agree to that while A1 exists. B3 is the mirror image. Both are pushed towards either betrayal or cooperating with their own set, and either is a good candidate for the isolated-team conversation.
- **Sabotage is worth organising.** Breaking a soft goal is cheap — one object in the wrong place — and it can cost the victim 2 points rather than 1. Three teams in a set can agree to protect the three goals they care about and spoil the fourth. It tends to appear in round 3 once the scoreboard has shown who is ahead.
- **Total peace is possible, and nothing rewards it.** Set A's plant goal and set B's personal-items goal both ask for corner 1 to hold their objects and no furniture, so the two can share the corner. That is what makes the 5 – 5 pair exist. What stands in the way is a practical squeeze: full peace needs three walls clear, the fourth holding only the stationery, the middle clear and no furniture in corner 1, which leaves every table and chair standing in the open floor. It holds, but no team gains by it and one team moving ends it.

## The arithmetic

Worth having in your head, because teams will ask afterwards whether they played well.

**Is betrayal worth it?** Cooperating pays a certain 3. Betrayal pays 5 if it lands, and if it fails you are one of the three teams on 0, 1 or 2 — call it 1 on average. So betrayal beats cooperating only if it lands often enough:

| If a failed betrayal leaves you with… | The betrayal has to land this often |
|---|---|
| 0 points | 60% |
| 1 point | 50% |
| 2 points | 33% |

With four teams wanting the same furniture and two pairs of hands each, a team going alone gets there roughly a quarter of the time. That is not close to any of those thresholds: a quarter of the time gives an average return of 2 against a steady 3. Betrayal on its own is the wrong play here, and teams will still try it.

**Is collusion worth it?** Collusion that pays 4 beats cooperating only if it holds two rounds in three. One that pays 5 needs to hold half the time. One that pays 3 never beats cooperating at all, which is why the A2 + B4 pair usually falls apart once both sides realise what it costs them.

Because the bar is that high, **teams have to pick one partner and keep their word.** Shopping several collusions at once and breaking most of them loses points. The mechanism enforces this as well as the arithmetic does: a team has one cheap goal to give up and one expensive one, so a second collusion costs double the first.

**Over four rounds**, with a collusion holding about 70% of the time:

| Strategy | Total out of 20 |
|---|---|
| Betray your set every round | 8 |
| Cooperate with your own set, every round, nobody breaks ranks | 12 |
| Run a 4-point collusion | 12.4 |
| Run the 5-point collusion | 15.2 |

The 12 for cooperating is fragile: it only holds while nobody in either set betrays it, and the moment one team in each set does, the cooperators fall to about 1 a round. That is the tension the whole version runs on. Cooperating is genuinely the best safe play and genuinely cannot survive contact with two teams who have found each other.

## Room setup

Tape five signs up before the session: **BACK WALL**, **WINDOW WALL**, **DOOR WALL**, **SIDE WALL**, and **CORNER 1** in any one corner.

Start the room so that none of the goals is already true:

- Chairs standing against all four walls
- At least one table in the middle of the room
- All three kinds of loose object spread around the room rather than gathered, and none of them in corner 1

Check this before teams enter. A goal that is true at the start hands out free points in round 1.

## Scoring

The facilitator and the assistant walk the room together at the end of each round's action phase and score all 24 goals. Every goal is a yes or a no, judged on what is in the room at that moment. Measurements are by eye, counted in paces.

Read the running totals out to the room after each round. They say less than teams will assume:

| Score | What it can mean |
|---|---|
| 5 | betrayed the set and kept everything |
| 4 | betrayed the set and gave up a 1-point goal — collusion across the sets |
| 3 | **either** a team that cooperated with its own set **or** a team that betrayed its set and lost a 2-point goal |
| 2 | one 2-point goal only, or a betrayal that cost both other goals |
| 1 | one 1-point goal only |

The ambiguous number is 3, which is also what cooperating pays — so a team can try a betrayal, half fail, and read on the board as a loyal member of its set. Betrayal has cover in this version, and teams that want to punish it have to go on what they saw in the room rather than what they heard read out.

One reading is unambiguous: **two teams both showing 5 in the same round can only be A1 and B1.**

Keep a scoring card per team: three tick boxes per round, four rounds.

## What to tell teams

- Their own slip, and that goals are scored every round and added up.
- Talking to other teams is allowed at any point. So is showing another team your slip, and so is lying about what is on it.
- Nothing said between teams binds anyone.
- The two scoring rules above about corner 1 and about lines along walls.

Nobody is told how many teams share their set, or that sets exist at all, or that the same goal can be worth different amounts to different teams.

The points live on the paper slip, so each team decides for itself whether to type them into its LLM. Some will, and their LLM will start ranking the goals for them. Watch for it — the moment a team hands over its priorities is worth raising in the debrief.

## Fewer than eight teams

Keep at least three teams in the larger set, or betrayal becomes the correct play and cooperation never forms.

| Teams | Use |
|---|---|
| 8 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2, B3, B4 |
| 6 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2 |
| 5 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1 |
| 4 | A1, A2, A3, B1 |
| 3 | A1, A2, A3 |

A1 and B1 are in every configuration down to four teams, so the 5 – 5 pair survives the cuts. At three teams there is no set B and no collusion layer at all, which leaves a much plainer game.

## Running with the final state only

To cut the scoring work, score once on the final room state instead of every round. It costs more here than it looks: the maximum drops to 5, and 3 is both the cooperative score and the most common one, so ties are very likely. Agree a tiebreak before you start. Rounds 1 to 3 stop paying anything, which also removes the round-3 turn against whoever is leading — one of the better things this version produces.
