# Point Systems Compared — 3-1-1, 2-2-1 and Mixed

Three scored versions of the game exist, and each has its own facilitator guide that stands on its own:

- **3-1-1** — [motivation_set_311points.md](motivation_set_311points.md)
- **2-2-1** — [motivation_set_221points.md](motivation_set_221points.md)
- **Mixed** — [motivation_set_mixedpoints.md](motivation_set_mixedpoints.md), which scores set A on 3-1-1 and set B on 2-2-1 in the same room at the same time

You do not need this page to run any of them. It is here for choosing between them, and for the debrief, where the difference between them is itself worth talking about.

All three use the same room, the same five signs, the same eight teams and the same eight roles. The round structure, the kit, the fractions of tables and chairs, and the room setup are identical. What changes is how the three goals on each slip are weighted, and three small pieces of goal wording that follow from it.

## The one-line difference

Each team has one goal that claims two areas of the room and demands sole use of them. All three guides call it the **betrayal goal**, because the team that takes it does so at the expense of the other three teams that want the same furniture. Teams are never told that name. In 3-1-1 they can at least see that one of their goals is worth more than the other two; in 2-2-1 nothing on a slip separates the two 2-point goals.

- **In 3-1-1 the betrayal goal is worth more than the team's other two goals put together.** Betrayal beats cooperating even when half of it fails.
- **In 2-2-1 the betrayal goal is worth less than the other two together.** A team that drops it and holds everything else beats a team that takes it and loses the rest.
- **In Mixed both are true at once, on opposite sides of the room.** Set A is weighted 3-1-1 and set B 2-2-1, so one set has no reason to hold together and the other has every reason to, and each has to reckon with the other.

Everything below follows from that.

## The arithmetic side by side

| | 3-1-1 | 2-2-1 | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everyone in a set cooperates | 2 each | **3 each** | 2 each in set A, 3 each in set B |
| Cooperating, as a share of the maximum | 40% | **60%** | 40% for set A, 60% for set B |
| One team betrays its set and it works | 5 | 5 | 5 |
| A betrayal that costs you both other goals | 3 — still beats cooperating | **2 — now loses to cooperating** | 3 in set A, 2 in set B |
| Cost of one betrayal to the whole set | 1 point | **4 points** | 1 point in set A, 4 in set B |
| Collusion across the sets | 4 | 4, sometimes 3, and once 5 | 3 to 5, and never below 3 for set A |
| Highest score two teams can hold at once | 5 and 4 | **5 and 5** | **5 and 5** |
| Total peace across all eight teams | impossible | possible, and pays every team 3 | **possible, but pays set A 2 and set B 3** |

In Mixed, each set keeps its own column in every table on this page: read the 3-1-1 figures for set A and the 2-2-1 figures for set B. What is new there is not the arithmetic inside a set but what the two sets do to each other.

**When is betrayal worth trying?** Betrayal pays 5 if it lands. If it fails you are one of the three teams left with the scraps. So it is worth trying only if it lands often enough:

| If a failed betrayal leaves you with… | 3-1-1 needs | 2-2-1 needs |
|---|---|---|
| 0 points | 40% | **60%** |
| 1 point | 25% | **50%** |
| 2 points | no threshold | 33% |

The blank row is not a rounding artefact. In 3-1-1 a team that attempts its betrayal goal and keeps both small goals scores 2, exactly what cooperating pays, so the attempt costs nothing and any chance of success makes it worth taking.

With four teams wanting the same furniture and two pairs of hands each, a team going alone gets there roughly a quarter of the time. In 3-1-1 that sits right on the line, which is why teams there keep trying it and are not simply wrong to. In 2-2-1 it is not close: a quarter of the time returns an average of 2 against a steady 3.

**When is collusion worth it?** Counting a failed collusion as 1 point either way:

| | 3-1-1 | 2-2-1 |
|---|---|---|
| A 4-point collusion has to hold | 1 round in 3 | **2 rounds in 3** |
| A 5-point collusion has to hold | 1 round in 4 | half the time |
| A 3-point collusion has to hold | half the time | **never worth it** |

This is the sharpest practical difference between the two versions. In 3-1-1 a team can shop several partners at once, break most of its promises and still come out ahead. In 2-2-1 that loses points: teams have to pick one partner and keep their word.

## What each version does to the room

**3-1-1** rewards going alone. Cooperation is the reasonable play on paper and rarely survives, because the first team to betray costs its set only 1 point and gains 3 for itself. Expect a competitive, noisy room, teams that keep trying betrayals they cannot finish, and promises made and broken freely. It is the better version for showing a group how incentives pull people apart.

**Mixed** puts one of each in the same room. Set A cooperates for 2 and can collude for 3, 4 or 5, and no collusion can leave it below 3, so it has nothing to gain by holding together and all four of its teams spend the game looking for a partner. Set B cooperates for a safe 3 and has to decide, four times over, whether an outside offer of 5 is worth breaking ranks for. The two sides also need a collusion to last for different lengths of time — set A needs a 5-point collusion to hold one round in four, set B needs the same one to hold half the time — so the side that profits by breaking its word is sitting opposite the side that needs it kept. Expect set A to promise widely and set B to be slow and suspicious. Set B also wins on points if nothing happens at all, 12 to 8, which is the thing to put to the room at the end.

**2-2-1** makes cooperation genuinely the best safe play, then makes it fragile. Cooperating pays 3 out of 5 and holds only while nobody in either set betrays it. A betrayal costs the set four times what it costs in 3-1-1, and it is harder to execute, because it is only worth attempting if a team can hold its other two goals at the same time — which splits two pairs of hands between taking ground and defending it. Expect slower, more verbal play, real cooperation, and sabotage: breaking a soft goal is cheap here and can cost the victim 2 points rather than 1.

## The weights on the slips

The betrayal goal is the 3-pointer in one version and a 2-pointer in the other. The change that does the work is on the other two goals: in 3-1-1 they are both worth 1 to everybody, and in 2-2-1 one of them is worth 2 and the other 1, and which is which differs by team.

| Team | Worth 2 in 2-2-1 | Worth 1 in 2-2-1 |
|---|---|---|
| A1 | plants in corner 1 | middle of the room empty |
| A2 | middle of the room empty | stationery under the windows |
| A3 | stationery under the windows | back wall clear |
| A4 | back wall clear | plants in corner 1 |
| B1 | clear path through the middle | personal items in corner 1 |
| B2 | door wall clear | clear path through the middle |
| B3 | side wall clear | door wall clear |
| B4 | personal items in corner 1 | side wall clear |

So in 2-2-1 the same goal is worth 2 points to one team and 1 point to another, and nothing on any slip says so. Teams find out only by talking, and it is the most useful thing they can learn about each other. In 3-1-1 there is nothing of the kind to discover: every soft goal is worth 1 to whoever holds it.

Mixed uses the 3-1-1 weights for set A's four slips and the 2-2-1 weights for set B's, unchanged. One consequence is worth knowing before you run it: the weights on a slip say which set it came from, so two teams comparing paper find out at once whether they are on the same side. There is no way to hide it while keeping both sets on a maximum of 5, and the version is better for it, because collusion across the sets is what it is for.

## Three wording changes

| Goal | 3-1-1 | 2-2-1 | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plants in corner 1 | "with nothing else in that corner" | "with no furniture in that corner" | the corner can be shared |
| Personal items in corner 1 | no exclusivity clause | "with no furniture in that corner" | the corner can be shared |
| Stationery under the windows | no wall clause | "with nothing else standing along the WINDOW WALL" | tables laid along the window wall now break it outright |

The corner is the structural one. In 3-1-1 set A's plant goal wants corner 1 holding plants and nothing else, while set B's personal-items goal wants the personal items piled in that same corner, so one of them always has to lose. Three of the possible pairs across the sets hang on who concedes it, and those are the collusions most likely to be struck and then broken. Total peace across all eight teams is impossible in every room, at any furniture count.

In 2-2-1 both goals exclude only furniture, so plants and personal items can share corner 1. That is what creates the 5 – 5 pair, and it makes total peace possible — though nothing rewards it, and one team moving ends it.

The 2-2-1 guide also states two judging rules in the briefing: corner 1 counts as its own area rather than part of the walls that meet there, and a line along a wall does not have to reach the corner. They are compatible with 3-1-1 and worth stating there too, but 2-2-1 leans on them much harder, because the shared corner only works if a pile standing in it does not count as standing against a wall.

Mixed uses the 2-2-1 wording and both judging rules throughout, on set A's slips as well as set B's. So the corner is shared there, the 5 – 5 pair exists, and total peace is reachable. It is reachable in a particular way: set A cooperating with itself and set B cooperating with itself do not obstruct each other anywhere, so peace is simply what happens when both sets cooperate at once, and nobody has to negotiate across the sets to get there. What stops it is that peace pays set A 2 a round and set B 3, so the room can reach its best collective result and still hand the game to one half of it.

## Collusion across the sets

| Pair | 3-1-1 | 2-2-1 | Mixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 + B1 | 5 – 4 or 4 – 5, turns on who takes corner 1 | **5 – 5** | **5 – 5** |
| A4 + B2 | 4 – 4 | 4 – 4 | 4 – 4 |
| A2 + B4 | 4 – 4 | 3 – 3 | 4 – 3 |
| A2 + B1 | 5 – 4 | 5 – 4 | 5 – 4 |
| A1 + B4 | 4 – 4 or 3 – 5 | 4 – 5 | 4 – 5 |
| A4 + B1 | 4 – 4 or 5 – 3 | 5 – 3 | 5 – 3 |
| A1 + B2 | 4 – 5 | 3 – 5 | 4 – 5 |
| A3 + B1 | 5 – 3 | 5 – 2 | 5 – 2 |
| A1 + B3 | 3 – 5 | 2 – 5 | 3 – 5 |

The same nine pairs are possible in all three versions; every other pair wants the same corner or the same middle and can never both hold. What differs is the terms. In 3-1-1 several pairs are even at 4 – 4 and three of them turn on a corner that cannot be shared. In 2-2-1 only one pair is cheap and even, one is even and expensive, and one gives both teams everything.

In Mixed, read the column down the set A side and one thing stands out: no team in set A ever scores below 3. Its two other goals are worth 1 each, so there is very little a partner can take from it, and colluding is safe for set A in a way it is not for anybody in either of the other versions. Read the set B side and the spread is 5, 5, 5, 5, 4, 4, 3, 3, 2 — collusion is worth a lot to some teams in set B and worse than doing nothing to others.

A1 and B1 are the pair to watch in every version, for different reasons. In 3-1-1 they have the most possible partners and the least to bargain with, because neither one's betrayal goal damages anything the other set wants. In 2-2-1 that same fact makes them each other's perfect partner and the only 5 – 5 in the game — and neither can know it without showing its slip. In Mixed it does both: A1 is the one team that can offer any team in set B a full 5, which is the only offer worth leaving set B's steady 3 for, so A1 decides which team in set B gets pulled out and B1 is the partner A1 does best with.

A3 and B3 have a hard game in every version. Each has one compatible partner, on terms that favour them heavily, and that partner has better options.

## What the scoreboard gives away

Read the running totals out after each round in either version. They do not say the same thing.

| Score | 3-1-1 | 2-2-1 |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | betrayal landed, kept everything | betrayal landed, kept everything |
| 4 | betrayal landed, gave up a small goal — collusion across the sets | betrayal landed, gave up a 1-point goal — collusion across the sets |
| 3 | betrayal landed, lost both small goals | **either** a cooperator **or** a betrayal that lost a 2-point goal |
| 2 | a cooperator, or a betrayal that failed cleanly | one 2-point goal only, or a betrayal that cost both other goals |
| 1 | one small goal only | one 1-point goal only |

In 3-1-1 no team can score above 2 without landing its betrayal goal, so anyone above 2 has betrayed its set and the whole room can see it. In 2-2-1 the ceiling for a cooperator is 3, which is also what a betraying team shows after losing a 2-point goal. Betrayal has cover in 2-2-1, and teams that want to punish it have to go on what they saw in the room rather than what they heard read out.

One reading in 2-2-1 is unambiguous: two teams both showing 5 in the same round can only be A1 and B1.

In Mixed each half of the room reads on its own column, so the same number means different things depending on which set the team is in. A 2 is what a cooperator in set A shows and what a badly broken team in set B shows; a 3 is what a cooperator in set B shows and what only a betrayal can produce in set A. The cutoffs are the useful part: **any team in set A showing 3 or more has landed its betrayal goal**, because set A cannot reach 3 without it, while in set B the cutoff is 4 and a 3 stays ambiguous. Two teams both showing 5 can still only be A1 and B1. And everyone on 2 or 3 with nobody above it means the room is at peace and does not know it.

## Choosing, and running both

**Run 3-1-1** to show a group how incentives break cooperation. Cooperating is the reasonable play and it will not hold, and the scoreboard names whoever broke it.

**Run 2-2-1** to test whether a group can find cooperation and keep its word. It rewards sticking to one collusion, punishes shopping around, and hides betrayal well enough that trust has to be built on what teams observe rather than on the numbers read out.

**Run Mixed** when you have one session rather than two and want the contrast to happen across the room instead of across two evenings. It is the only version where the two halves of the room are playing to different arithmetic, so a promise is worth keeping on one side and worth breaking on the other, and it is the only one where the best result available to everyone at once is reachable and unfairly shared. It asks more of the facilitator than the other two: you are scoring two systems at the same time, and the scoreboard means different things on each side. Bear in mind that no participant experiences both systems — only you see both, so the reveal at the end has to do that work.

**Running both with the same group:** run 3-1-1 first. A group that has played 2-2-1 will carry cautious, cooperative habits into 3-1-1, where those habits simply lose. A group that has played 3-1-1 has to unlearn most of what worked, which is the more interesting thing to watch and a good debrief in itself. Say nothing about the weights having changed; let them find it.

**Scoring on the final room state only,** to cut the work, costs more in 2-2-1: the maximum drops to 5 and 3 is both the cooperative score and the most common one, so ties are very likely and you should agree a tiebreak first. Mixed carries the same cost on its set B side, and loses the round-3 turn against whoever is leading along with it.
