# Motivation Sets — Mixed Point Version

A scored version of the game for 8 teams. It replaces the "no declared winner" rule; everything else about the game stays the same.

> **The rest of this page down to the cut line is for the facilitator.** Teams get their own slip and nothing else. The structure described here is what they have to work out for themselves during play.

---

## How the points work

Every team gets three goals on one slip, worth 5 points in total. A team with all three showing in the room scores 5 for that round. Goals are scored at the end of every round and added up across the game. Four rounds gives a maximum of 20.

There are two sets of four teams. **Set A moves the tables. Set B moves the chairs.** One goal on every slip claims two areas of the room and demands sole use of them. This guide calls it the **betrayal goal**, because the team that takes it does so at the expense of the other three teams that want the same furniture. Teams are never told that name. The other two goals ask for one area to be cleared or filled with small objects.

**The two sets are weighted differently, and nobody is told so.**

| | Set A | Set B |
|---|---|---|
| Betrayal goal | 3 points | 2 points |
| Other two goals | 1 point and 1 point | 2 points and 1 point |
| Slip total | 5 | 5 |

Both sets can score 5 in a round and 20 across the game, so the scoreboard compares them directly. What differs is what each set has to do to get there.

## What the weights do

In set A the betrayal goal is worth more than a team's other two goals put together, so taking it beats cooperating even when half of it fails. In set B it is worth less than the other two together, so a team that drops it and holds everything else beats a team that takes it and loses the rest.

| | Set A | Set B |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone in the set cooperates | 2 each, 8 for the set | **3 each, 12 for the set** |
| Cooperating, as a share of the maximum | 40% | **60%** |
| One team's betrayal goal lands | 5 for that team | 5 for that team |
| Where the other three land | 0, 1 and 1 in some order | 0, 1 and 2 in some order |
| Set total when one team betrays | 7 | 8 |
| Cost of one betrayal to the whole set | 1 point | **4 points** |

That difference decides how each set behaves, and the two behaviours are the version.

**Set A has no reason to hold together.** Cooperating pays 2. Every agreement it can reach with a team in the other set pays 3, 4 or 5, and none of them can leave a set A team below 3. On top of that, a betrayal attempt that fails while both small goals survive also pays 2 — the same as cooperating — so trying costs nothing. Expect all four teams in set A to spend the game looking for a partner in set B, and expect none of them to protect its own set.

**Set B has a lot to hold together for.** Cooperating pays a safe 3 out of 5. But there are four teams in the other set, each able to offer one team in set B a 5 for breaking ranks, and every one of them has good reason to try. Set B's problem is not betrayal from inside. It is the offer from outside.

The same goal is worth different amounts to different teams, and nothing on any slip says so. Teams find out only by talking, and it is the most useful thing they can learn about each other.

## Fitting the game to your room

The goals are written as fractions, so they carry across to any room and no number appears on any slip. Two numbers set themselves from whatever furniture you have.

**Tables.** Every set A betrayal goal asks for *half the tables* in one place and *the other half* in another, so it always uses every table in the room. That is what stops two teams in set A from both succeeding, whatever the count. An odd table goes in either group. Four tables or more works well; below that the lines are too short to read as anything.

**Chairs.** Every set B betrayal goal asks for *a third of the chairs* in one place and *another third* in another. Two teams doing that at once would need four thirds of the chairs, which no room has, so again only one of them can succeed. Round a third upwards.

| Chairs in the room | A third is | One betrayal goal needs | Two teams would need |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
| 15 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
| 18 | 6 | 12 | 24 |
| 20 | 7 | 14 | 28 |
| 24 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
| 30 | 10 | 20 | 40 |
| 36 | 12 | 24 | 48 |
| 40 | 14 | 28 | 56 |

Say both numbers out loud in the briefing and write them on the board: "there are 24 chairs, so a third is 8; there are 6 tables, so half is 3." Everyone needs to be working from the same figures, and neither figure gives anything away.

**Loose objects.** Plants, stationery and personal items. Any three kinds will do, as long as they are easy to tell apart by eye and there are several of each. Swap in bags, books, cups, cables, whatever the room holds. Keep it to three distinct kinds.

**Distances.** A pace stands in for a metre. In a small room use half a pace. The cleared middle needs to be big enough for four people to stand in without touching.

**Minimum kit.** Four tables, twelve chairs, three kinds of loose object. Below that there is too little to fight over.

**Floor space.** This version has an eight-team outcome in which every wall is clear and all the furniture stands in the open floor. Before the session, push every table and chair off all four walls, out of the middle and out of corner 1, and see whether it fits. If it does not, that outcome is out of reach whatever the teams decide, and you should know that going in.

**Large rooms.** A fraction holds its difficulty steady against the room, so the work grows as the room does. Past roughly 36 chairs or 8 tables, a betrayal goal asks for more furniture than two pairs of hands can shift in six minutes, and it starts failing on the clock alone rather than on anything a team decided.

Fix that by taking furniture out of play and leaving the fractions alone. Move the surplus out of the room, then announce what is left: a third of 24 chairs plays exactly like a third of 60. Shrinking the fraction instead breaks the game, because the rule that stops two teams in a set from both winning needs the fraction to stay above a quarter, and a quarter exactly fails at every count that divides by four.

### Two judging rules to state in the briefing

These decide a lot of close calls.

**Corner 1 is its own area.** Something standing in corner 1 does not count as standing against either of the walls that meet there. So a pile of plants in corner 1 does not break a goal asking for a clear back wall.

**A line or row along a wall does not have to reach the corner.** A team may stop its line a pace short, and a goal asking for nothing within one pace of that line is judged against the line as laid, not against the whole wall. Goals that say *along its whole length* do mean the whole wall, corner included.

## The hidden structure

### Inside set A

The four betrayal goals want the same tables in four different places, so only one team in set A can have its own. Each team's two small goals survive its own betrayal goal and are broken by its rivals'.

- **All four drop their betrayal goal and help each other.** Every team gets both small goals. **2 points each, 8 for the set.**
- **One team pushes its betrayal goal through and the others give way.** **5 for that team**, and 0, 1 and 1 for the rest. **7 for the set.**

Betrayal costs the set only 1 point and pays the team that does it 3 more than cooperating. Nothing here holds set A together, and nothing is meant to.

### Inside set B

The same geometry, on the chairs, with different weights.

- **All four drop their betrayal goal and help each other.** **3 points each, 12 for the set.**
- **One team pushes its betrayal goal through and the others give way.** **5 for that team**, and 0, 1 and 2 for the rest. **8 for the set.**

So a betrayal in set B moves 4 points out of the set and hands 2 of them to the team that did it. The damage is four times what the same act costs set A, and it shows up on the scoreboard the same evening.

### Across the sets

Set A claims the back wall, the window wall, corner 1 and the middle. Set B claims the door wall, the side wall, corner 1 and the middle. Because the two sets meet only in corner 1 and the middle, a team from A and a team from B can often hold their betrayal goals at the same time, each giving up whichever of its own goals the other's betrayal goal destroys. This guide calls that **collusion**.

What each team gives up is fixed by the geometry. What it costs is not, and it costs the two sets differently: a team in set A can lose only 1-point goals, so no collusion can take it below 3, while a team in set B can lose a 2-point goal and land on 2.

This is the full table of pairs that can both hold their betrayal goals:

| Pair | Score | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| A1 + B1 | **5 – 5** | neither gives up anything |
| A2 + B1 | 5 – 4 | A2 gives up nothing; B1 loses its 1-point goal |
| A1 + B2 | 4 – 5 | B2 gives up nothing; A1 loses a 1-point goal |
| A1 + B4 | 4 – 5 | B4 gives up nothing; A1 loses a 1-point goal |
| A4 + B2 | 4 – 4 | one goal each |
| A2 + B4 | 4 – 3 | A2 loses a 1-pointer, B4 loses its 2-pointer |
| A4 + B1 | 5 – 3 | A4 gives up nothing; B1 loses its 2-point goal |
| A1 + B3 | 3 – 5 | B3 gives up nothing; A1 loses both its small goals |
| A3 + B1 | 5 – 2 | A3 gives up nothing; B1 loses both |

Every other pair (A2+B2, A2+B3, A3+B2, A3+B3, A3+B4, A4+B3, A4+B4) wants the same corner or the same middle, so those two teams can never both hold their betrayal goals.

Only one team per set can hold its betrayal goal at a time, so **at most one of these pairs can be live in any round.** Being the team in your set that gets the collusion is the thing worth competing for.

### Six things worth watching for

- **A1 can pay every team in set B a full 5.** A1's betrayal goal claims the back wall and the window wall, which no team in set B wants, and A1 carries the damage itself because both its small goals are worth only 1. A1 scores 5, 4, 3 and 4 across its four possible partners, and the partner scores 5 every time. No other team can make that offer to more than one team, and A1 can choose which of the four to approach.

- **Only three teams can pull a team out of set B.** A team in set B leaves a certain 3 only for a 4 or a 5. That is A1 to any of the four at 5, A2 to B1 at 4, and A4 to B2 at 4. Everything else on the table pays a team in set B 3 or less, which is what it already has for doing nothing. So set B is not under general pressure. It is under pressure from one team at a time, and it only has to say no.

- **The two sides of a collusion need it to last for different lengths of time.** Counting a collapsed collusion as 1 point either way, a team in set A needs a 5-point collusion to hold one round in four, and a 4-point one to hold one round in three. A team in set B needs the same collusions to hold half the time and two rounds in three. So set A can promise several teams at once, break most of its word and still come out ahead, while set B has to pick one partner and keep faith. Neither can see the other's slip unless it is shown. This is the pressure the whole version runs on, and it is worth naming in the debrief: the side that gains by breaking its word is sitting opposite the side that needs it kept.

- **A1 and B1 are each other's best partner, and neither can know it without showing its slip.** A1's betrayal goal breaks nothing set B wants, B1's breaks nothing set A wants, and their four remaining goals all hold together. It is the only 5 – 5 in the game. Both will spend the game talking, and both do worse the later they find each other: A1 scores 5, 4, 4 and 3 across its four possible partners, and B1 scores 5, 4, 3 and 2 across its own. This is the one place where showing another team the real slip pays better than lying about it.

- **A3 and B3 have a hard game, for opposite reasons.** A3's only compatible partner is B1, at 5 – 2, and B1 will not take a 2 while a 3 is available for doing nothing and a 5 is available elsewhere: A3 has nothing anyone will accept. B3's only compatible partner is A1, at 3 – 5 in B3's favour, and A1 does better with B1, B2 or B4 and will go there first: B3 is approached last, if at all. Both are pushed back on their own set, and A3 is the better candidate for the isolated-team conversation in the debrief.

- **Sabotage is worth organising in set B and not in set A.** Breaking one of set B's goals is cheap — one object in the wrong place — and can cost the victim 2 points. Three teams in set B can agree to protect the three goals they care about and spoil the fourth. In set A the same act costs the victim 1 and is rarely worth the hands.

## Total peace

Every goal in this version can be true at once. Three walls clear, the fourth holding only the stationery, the middle clear, and plants and personal items sharing corner 1 with no furniture in it. Every table and chair ends up standing in the open floor.

Nothing about it requires anyone to talk across the sets. If set A cooperates with itself and set B cooperates with itself, the two halves fit together on their own, and total peace is what the room ends up in without anybody having aimed at it.

It pays the room 20 points: 2 to each team in set A and 3 to each in set B. The strongest collusion in the game, A1 and B1 both landing their betrayal goals, pays those two teams 10 and leaves 15 in the room. So the best thing available to everyone at once is worth more than the best thing available to anybody in particular, and it is reachable.

Three things stand against it, and all three are worth raising in the debrief.

**It does not pay the two sets the same.** Peace pays every team in set A 2 and every team in set B 3. Over four rounds that is 8 against 12. A room that simply keeps the peace hands the game to set B, and a team in set A that holds the peace is choosing to come second. The cooperative outcome is available and it is not fair, which is a harder and more useful point than either half on its own.

**Four teams are paid to end it.** Every collusion open to a team in set A pays 3, 4 or 5 against the 2 that peace pays, and no collusion can leave a set A team worse than 3. Peace therefore has four teams standing to gain by breaking it and no team in set A standing to lose. It takes two, though: a team in set A cannot collude without a team in set B agreeing to take its betrayal goal, so set B ends the peace or it holds.

**Nobody in the room can see it happen.** No team knows the other slips or that sets exist. What the room sees is four teams on 2 and four on 3, with nobody above 3. Teams may well read that as nobody winning. Peace is yours to notice and yours to reveal.

## The arithmetic

Worth having in your head, because teams will ask afterwards whether they played well.

**Is betrayal on its own worth it?** It pays 5 if it lands. If it fails you are one of the three teams left with the scraps. So it is worth trying only if it lands often enough:

| If a failed betrayal leaves you with… | Set A needs | Set B needs |
|---|---|---|
| 0 points | 40% | 60% |
| 1 point | 25% | 50% |
| 2 points | no threshold | 33% |

The blank is not a rounding artefact. A team in set A that attempts its betrayal goal and keeps both small goals scores 2, exactly what cooperating pays it, so the attempt costs nothing and any chance at all makes it worth taking.

With four teams wanting the same furniture and two pairs of hands each, a team going alone gets there roughly a quarter of the time. For set A that sits right on the line, which is why those teams keep trying and are not simply wrong to. For set B it is not close: a quarter of the time returns an average of 2 against a steady 3.

**Is collusion worth it?** Counting a failed collusion as 1 point:

| Collusion paying | Set A needs it to hold | Set B needs it to hold |
|---|---|---|
| 5 points | 1 round in 4 | half the time |
| 4 points | 1 round in 3 | 2 rounds in 3 |
| 3 points | half the time | never worth it |

**Over four rounds**, with a collusion holding about 70% of the time:

| Strategy | Set A, out of 20 | Set B, out of 20 |
|---|---|---|
| Betray your set every round | 8 | 8 |
| Cooperate with your own set, nobody breaking ranks | 8 | 12 |
| Run a 4-point collusion | 12.4 | 12.4 |
| Run the 5-point collusion | 15.2 | 15.2 |

Read the two columns against each other. Collusion is worth the same to both sets, but it replaces 8 for a team in set A and only 12 for a team in set B. That is the whole engine: the same offer is transformative on one side of the room and marginal on the other, and the side it barely helps is the side that has to agree to it.

## Room setup

Tape five signs up before the session: **BACK WALL**, **WINDOW WALL**, **DOOR WALL**, **SIDE WALL**, and **CORNER 1** in any one corner.

Start the room so that none of the goals is already true:

- Chairs standing against all four walls
- At least one table in the middle of the room
- All three kinds of loose object spread around the room rather than gathered, and none of them in corner 1

Check this before teams enter. A goal that is true at the start hands out free points in round 1.

## Scoring

The facilitator and the assistant walk the room together at the end of each round's action phase and score all 24 goals. Every goal is a yes or a no, judged on what is in the room at that moment. Measurements are by eye, counted in paces.

Keep a scoring card per team: three tick boxes per round, four rounds.

Read the running totals out after each round. The same number means different things on the two sides of the room, so keep this table to hand.

| Score | A team in set A | A team in set B |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | betrayal goal landed, kept both small goals | betrayal goal landed, kept everything |
| 4 | betrayal goal landed, gave up a small goal — collusion | betrayal goal landed, gave up its 1-point goal — collusion |
| 3 | betrayal goal landed, lost both small goals | **cooperated with its own set**, or betrayed and lost a 2-point goal |
| 2 | **cooperated with its own set**, or an attempt that failed with both small goals standing | one 2-point goal only, or a betrayal that cost both other goals |
| 1 | one small goal only | one 1-point goal only |
| 0 | its set's betrayal goal went to someone else | its set's betrayal goal went to someone else |

Three readings are worth having on the night:

- **Any team in set A showing 3 or more has landed its betrayal goal.** Without it, set A tops out at 2. There is no cover for it on that side of the room.
- **Any team in set B showing 4 or more has landed its betrayal goal.** A 3 is the ambiguous number there: it is what cooperating pays, and also what a betrayal that lost a 2-point goal pays. A team in set B can try a betrayal, half fail, and read on the board as a loyal member of its set.
- **Two teams both showing 5 in the same round can only be A1 and B1**, whether or not they agreed on it.

Everyone on 2 or 3 and nobody above it means the room is at peace and does not know it.

## What to tell teams

- Their own slip, and that goals are scored every round and added up.
- Talking to other teams is allowed at any point. So is showing another team your slip, and so is lying about what is on it.
- Nothing said between teams binds anyone.
- The two judging rules above, about corner 1 and about lines along walls.

Nobody is told how many teams share their set, or that sets exist at all, or that the two sets are scored differently.

That last one will not survive contact. A slip weighted 3-1-1 and a slip weighted 2-2-1 are visibly from different sets, so two teams comparing paper learn immediately that they are not on the same side. Let it happen. Collusion across the sets is what this version is for, and anything that speeds up finding a partner inside a six-minute round helps. It also gives a team in set B a way to check that a claimed partner really is from the other set, which slightly protects the side that needs protecting.

The points live on the paper slip, so each team decides for itself whether to type them into its LLM. Some will, and their LLM will start ranking the goals for them. Watch for it — the moment a team hands over its priorities is worth raising in the debrief.

## Fewer than eight teams

Keep at least three teams in set B. Set B holding together against the offer from outside is what this version is about, and below three there is nothing to hold together. A1 and B1 stay in every configuration, so the 5 – 5 survives the cuts.

| Teams | Use |
|---|---|
| 8 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2, B3, B4 |
| 7 | A1, A2, A4, B1, B2, B3, B4 |
| 6 | A1, A2, A4, B1, B2, B3 |
| 5 | A1, A2, B1, B2, B3 |

Five teams is the floor. Below that one team in set A takes its betrayal goal unopposed every round and there is no game left in it.

## Running with the final state only

To cut the scoring work, score once on the final room state instead of every round. The maximum drops to 5 and ties become likely, so agree a tiebreak first. Rounds 1 to 3 stop paying anything, which pushes all the bargaining and all the furniture-moving into the last round, and removes the turn against whoever is leading that tends to arrive in round 3. The slips work unchanged either way.
