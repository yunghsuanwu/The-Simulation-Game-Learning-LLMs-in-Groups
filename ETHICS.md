# Ethics

## Consent

Before any instruction begins, participants know the session will be
highly interactive and intensive, and that it deals with technology.
Before play starts they are walked through the roles and the rounds,
and they type in the opening prompts themselves, reading the prompts
is part of how they come to understand what they're agreeing to take
part in.

The game requires physical movement, and participants are told this
upfront. Adjustments are available for teams that include people with
physical disabilities, and the restrictions built into the roles are
explained before play begins.

In most settings there will be some situational pressure to take part
— a manager booked the workshop, colleagues are watching. Facilitators
should acknowledge this by signalling clearly, before the game begins,
that the session is quite intense and that anyone can take a step back
at any point. If someone leaves the room or sits back during play, the
facilitator must not stop them or draw attention to it.

The game uses incomplete disclosure: participants are not told the full
framing upfront. The instructions are deliberately evasive in a way
that makes it clear something is going on, rather than pretending
otherwise. The conceit is always revealed in the debrief, in the same
session. This reveal is not optional. If a run has to be cut short,
the facilitator still does the reveal before people leave.

## Photography

Photos may be taken during play, but this depends on the host
organisation and the agreement it has with its participants. The
session usually runs as part of a wider programme with a pre-set
photography policy, which the game inherits.

Where no such policy exists, participants must be alerted that photos
will be taken and told they can contact the host to opt out.

Photos taken from the facilitation side are not used for social media
or marketing — they exist to help understand the game afterwards. Where
the host institution takes its own photos, the facilitator has no
control over how those are used, and participants should direct any
concerns about them to the host.

## Data handling

At present, the only thing collected from a run is a short reflection
written by the facilitator. No names are attached. These reflections
begin life in the facilitator's private notes and end up in this
repository (see RUNS.md), where they are used to iterate on the game's
design.

Photos taken from the facilitation side (see Photography) are kept
privately for the same purpose (understanding and improving the game)
and are not published.

It is possible the game will develop to collect more information in
future — for example structured observer notes or participant surveys.
If and when that happens, a deletion and retention policy will be
implemented and this section developed alongside it.

## Reporting runs responsibly

No identifying information should appear in a public run report: no
participant names, and nothing that could identify an individual
through what they said or did.

Run records should be as honest as possible, noting failures as well as
successes. What went wrong is essential information for the development
of the game.

Run reports are aggregate and brief enough that they don't require
disclosure to participants, but facilitators are welcome to point
participants to this repository so they can see the project and the
report of their own session for themselves.
