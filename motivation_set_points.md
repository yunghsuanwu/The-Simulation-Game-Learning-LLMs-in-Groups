# Motivation Sets — Point Version

A scored version of the game for 8 teams. It replaces the "no declared winner" rule; everything else about the game stays the same.

> **The rest of this page down to the cut line is for the facilitator.** Teams get their own slip and nothing else. The structure described here is what they have to work out for themselves during play.

---

## How the points work

Every team gets three goals on one slip: one worth **3 points** and two worth **1 point** each. A team that has all three showing in the room scores 5 for that round.

Goals are scored at the end of every round and added up across the game. Four rounds gives a maximum of 20.

There are two sets of four teams. **Set A moves the tables. Set B moves the chairs.** Every 3-point goal claims two areas of the room and demands sole use of them. Every 1-point goal asks for one area to be cleared or filled with small objects.

## Fitting the game to your room

The goals are written as fractions, so they carry across to any room and no number appears on any slip. Two numbers set themselves from whatever furniture you have.

**Tables.** Every set A 3-point goal asks for *half the tables* in one place and *the other half* in another, so it always uses every table in the room. That is what stops two teams in set A from both succeeding, whatever the count. An odd table goes in either group. Four tables or more works well; below that the lines are too short to read as anything.

**Chairs.** Every set B 3-point goal asks for *a third of the chairs* in one place and *another third* in another. Two teams doing that at once would need four thirds of the chairs, which no room has, so again only one of them can succeed. Round a third upwards.

| Chairs in the room | A third is | One 3-point goal needs | Two teams would need |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
| 15 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
| 18 | 6 | 12 | 24 |
| 20 | 7 | 14 | 28 |
| 24 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
| 30 | 10 | 20 | 40 |
| 36 | 12 | 24 | 48 |
| 40 | 14 | 28 | 56 |

Say both numbers out loud in the briefing and write them on the board: "there are 24 chairs, so a third is 8; there are 6 tables, so half is 3." Everyone needs to be working from the same figures, and neither figure gives anything away.

**Loose objects.** Three of the 1-point goals each claim a different kind of loose object: plants, stationery, personal items. Any three kinds will do, as long as they are easy to tell apart by eye and there are several of each. Swap in bags, books, cups, cables, whatever the room holds. Keep it to three distinct kinds, because two of them are what makes corner 1 impossible to share.

**Distances.** A pace stands in for a metre. In a small room use half a pace. The cleared middle needs to be big enough for four people to stand in without touching.

**Minimum kit.** Four tables, twelve chairs, three kinds of loose object. Below that there is too little to fight over.

**Large rooms.** A fraction holds its difficulty steady against the room, so the work grows as the room does. Past roughly 36 chairs or 8 tables, a 3-point goal asks for more furniture than two pairs of hands can shift in six minutes, and it starts failing on the clock alone rather than on anything a team decided.

Fix that by taking furniture out of play, leaving the fractions alone. Move the surplus out of the room, then announce what is left: a third of 24 chairs plays exactly like a third of 60. Shrinking the fraction instead breaks the game. The rule that stops two teams in a set from both winning needs the fraction to stay above a quarter, and a quarter exactly fails at every count that divides by four.

## The hidden structure

### Inside a set

The four 3-point goals in a set want the same furniture in four different places, so only one team in a set can have theirs. Each team's two 1-point goals survive its own 3-point goal and get broken by the other three teams' 3-point goals.

That produces two outcomes:

- **All four teams drop their 3-point goal and help each other.** Every team gets both its 1-point goals. **2 points each, 8 for the set.**
- **One team pushes its 3-point goal through and the others give way.** **5 points for that team**, 1 or 0 for each of the others, 7 for the set.

Betrayal pays 5 against 2, which makes it look like the obvious move. It only pays if the other three give way. With four teams wanting the same space and two pairs of hands each, a team going alone gets there roughly a quarter of the time, so the average return on betrayal is about 1.25 against a steady 2. Teams that reason it through should cooperate. Teams that back their own arms will try it anyway, and two teams trying it at once usually leaves both with nothing.

### Across the sets

Set A claims the back wall, the window wall, corner 1 and the middle. Set B claims the door wall, the side wall, corner 1 and the middle. The two sets meet in **corner 1 and the middle of the room**, so a team from A and a team from B can often hold their 3-point goals at the same time, each giving up one 1-point goal. That is 4 points each — double what cooperating with your own set pays, and it requires agreement with one team instead of three.

The catch is that few pairs are actually even. This is the full table of pairs that can both succeed:

| Pair | Score | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| A2 + B4 | 4 – 4 | even |
| A4 + B2 | 4 – 4 | even |
| A1 + B4 | 4 – 4 or 3 – 5 | turns on who takes corner 1 |
| A4 + B1 | 4 – 4 or 5 – 3 | turns on who takes corner 1 |
| A1 + B1 | 5 – 4 or 4 – 5 | turns on who takes corner 1 |
| A2 + B1 | 5 – 4 | A2 does better |
| A1 + B2 | 4 – 5 | B2 does better |
| A3 + B1 | 5 – 3 | A3 does much better |
| A1 + B3 | 3 – 5 | B3 does much better |

Every other pair (A2+B2, A2+B3, A3+B2, A3+B3, A3+B4, A4+B3, A4+B4) wants the same corner or the same middle, so those two teams can never both succeed.

Three pairs hang on corner 1, where set A's plant goal and set B's personal-items goal want the same space on terms that cannot both hold. Whoever concedes the corner drops a point while the other takes 5. Those are the deals most likely to be struck and then broken.

Three things worth watching for:

- **The teams with the most partners have the least to bargain with.** A1 and B1 can each pair with any of the four teams in the other set, and both come off worse in most of those deals, because neither one's 3-point goal damages anything the other set wants. A3 and B3 are the mirror image: one possible partner each, on terms of 5–3 in their own favour. Expect A1 and B1 to spend the game talking, and expect A3 and B3 to be courted while having nothing to trade except holding back.
- **A3 and B3 can never get an even deal.** A3's only compatible partner is B1; B3's only compatible partner is A1. Both are pushed towards either predation or cooperating with their own set. These two teams are most likely to have a hard game, and either one is a good candidate for the isolated-team conversation in the debrief.
- **Total peace across all eight teams is impossible.** Set A's plant goal wants corner 1 holding plants and nothing else, while set B's personal-items goal wants the personal items piled in that same corner. One of them has to lose, in every room, at any furniture count. On top of that, three of the four walls get cleared if both sets cooperate fully, which leaves the chairs almost nowhere to stand. Something always gives.

## Room setup

Tape five signs up before the session: **BACK WALL**, **WINDOW WALL**, **DOOR WALL**, **SIDE WALL**, and **CORNER 1** in any one corner.

Start the room so that none of the goals is already true:

- Chairs standing against all four walls
- At least one table in the middle of the room
- All three kinds of loose object spread around the room rather than gathered, and none of them in corner 1

Check this before teams enter. A goal that is true at the start hands out free points in round 1.

## Scoring

The facilitator and the assistant walk the room together at the end of each round's action phase and score all 24 goals. Every goal is a yes or a no, judged on what is in the room at that moment. Measurements are by eye, counted in paces.

Read the running totals out to the room after each round. The totals leak information on purpose. A team showing 5 has just betrayed its own set, and a team showing 4 has usually done a deal across the sets, so the scoreboard tells everyone roughly who has done what without naming anyone.

Keep a scoring card per team: three tick boxes per round, four rounds.

## What to tell teams

- Their own slip, and that goals are scored every round and added up.
- Talking to other teams is allowed at any point. So is showing another team your slip, and so is lying about what is on it.
- Nothing said between teams binds anyone.

Nobody is told how many teams share their set, or that sets exist at all.

The points live on the paper slip, so each team decides for itself whether to type them into its LLM. Some will, and their LLM will start ranking the goals for them. Watch for it — the moment a team hands over its priorities is worth raising in the debrief.

## Fewer than eight teams

Keep at least three teams in the larger set, or betrayal becomes the correct play and no coalition ever forms.

| Teams | Use |
|---|---|
| 8 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2, B3, B4 |
| 6 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2 |
| 5 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1 |
| 4 | A1, A2, A3, B1 |
| 3 | A1, A2, A3 |

## Running with the final state only

To cut the scoring work, score once on the final room state instead of every round. The maximum drops to 5 and ties become likely, so agree a tiebreak first. Rounds 1 to 3 stop paying anything, which pushes all the bargaining and all the furniture-moving into the last round. The slips work unchanged either way.
