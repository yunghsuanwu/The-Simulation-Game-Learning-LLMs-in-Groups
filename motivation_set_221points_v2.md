# Motivation Sets — 2-2-1 Point Version

A scored version of the game for 8 teams. Points are added; there is no declared winner. Each team is working towards its own goals, and its points show how far it got. Everything else about the game stays the same.

> **The rest of this page down to the cut line is for the facilitator.** Teams get their own slip and nothing else. The structure described here is what they have to work out for themselves during play.

---

## How the points work

Every team gets three goals on one slip: two worth **2 points** and one worth **1 point**. A team with all three showing in the room scores 5 for that round.

Goals are scored at the end of every round and added up across the game. Four rounds gives a maximum of 20.

There are two sets of four teams. **Set A moves the tables. Set B moves the chairs.** One goal on every slip arranges the set's furniture across two areas of the room and needs sole use of them. This guide calls it the **furniture goal**. Teams are never told that name, and nothing on a slip separates the two 2-point goals. The other two goals each ask for one area to be cleared or filled with small objects.

## What the weights do

The furniture goal is worth 2, and a team's other two goals are worth 3 between them. So a team that sets its furniture goal aside and holds everything else scores more than a team that meets it and loses the rest. That single fact shapes the whole game:

| What happens | Points the team scores |
|---|---|
| Everyone in its set cooperates | **3** |
| It goes it alone and meets its furniture goal | 5 |
| It meets its furniture goal at the cost of both other goals | **2 — less than cooperating** |
| Another team in its set goes it alone and meets its furniture goal | 2, 1 or 0 |
| It colludes with a team from the other set | anywhere from 2 to 5, depending on the pair |

Counted across the whole set, one team going it alone costs a lot: the set scores 8 instead of the 12 it scores when everyone cooperates.

Cooperating pays 60% of the maximum, so a team has a lot to lose by gambling. Going it alone is also hard to carry out, because it is only worth attempting if you can hold your other two goals at the same time — which splits two pairs of hands between moving furniture and keeping your other goals intact.

The same goal among the other two is worth 2 points to one team and 1 point to another. Nothing on any slip says so. Teams find out only by talking, and it is the single most useful thing they can learn about each other.

## Fitting the game to your room

The goals are written as fractions, so they carry across to any room and no number appears on any slip. Two numbers set themselves from whatever furniture you have.

**Tables.** Every set A furniture goal asks for *half the tables* in one place and *the other half* in another, so it always uses every table in the room. That is what stops two teams in set A from both meeting theirs, whatever the count. An odd table goes in either group. Four tables or more works well; below that the lines are too short to read as anything.

**Chairs.** Every set B furniture goal asks for *a third of the chairs* in one place and *another third* in another. Two teams doing that at once would need four thirds of the chairs, which no room has, so again only one of them can be met. Round a third upwards.

| Chairs in the room | A third is | One furniture goal needs | Two teams would need |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 4 | 8 | 16 |
| 15 | 5 | 10 | 20 |
| 18 | 6 | 12 | 24 |
| 20 | 7 | 14 | 28 |
| 24 | 8 | 16 | 32 |
| 30 | 10 | 20 | 40 |
| 36 | 12 | 24 | 48 |
| 40 | 14 | 28 | 56 |

Say both numbers out loud in the briefing and write them on the board: "there are 24 chairs, so a third is 8; there are 6 tables, so half is 3." Everyone needs to be working from the same figures, and neither figure gives anything away.

**Loose objects.** Three of the goals each claim a different kind of loose object: plants, stationery, personal items. Any three kinds will do, as long as they are easy to tell apart by eye and there are several of each. Swap in bags, books, cups, cables, whatever the room holds. Keep it to three distinct kinds.

**Distances.** A pace stands in for a metre. In a small room use half a pace. The cleared middle needs to be big enough for four people to stand in without touching.

**Minimum kit.** Four tables, twelve chairs, three kinds of loose object. Below that there is too little to work with.

**Large rooms.** A fraction holds its difficulty steady against the room, so the work grows as the room does. Past roughly 36 chairs or 8 tables, a furniture goal asks for more furniture than two pairs of hands can shift in six minutes, and it starts failing on the clock alone rather than on anything a team decided.

Fix that by taking furniture out of play and leaving the fractions alone. Move the surplus out of the room, then announce what is left: a third of 24 chairs plays exactly like a third of 60. Shrinking the fraction instead breaks the game, because the rule that stops two teams in a set from both meeting their furniture goals needs the fraction to stay above a quarter, and a quarter exactly fails at every count that divides by four.

### Two scoring rules to state in the briefing

These decide a lot of close calls, and this version leans on them hard.

**Corner 1 is its own area.** Something standing in corner 1 does not count as standing against either of the walls that meet there. So a pile of plants in corner 1 does not break a goal asking for a clear back wall.

**A line or row along a wall does not have to reach the corner.** A team may stop its line a pace short, and a goal asking for nothing within one pace of that line is judged against the line as laid, not against the whole wall. Goals that say *along its whole length* do mean the whole wall, corner included.

## The hidden structure

### Inside a set

The four furniture goals in a set want the same furniture in four different places, so only one of them can be met at a time. Each team's other two goals survive its own furniture goal and are broken by the other three teams' furniture goals.

That produces two outcomes:

- **All four teams set their furniture goal aside and help each other.** Every team gets both its other goals. **3 points each, 12 for the set.**
- **One team meets its furniture goal and the others make room for it.** **5 points for that team.** Whichever team it is, the other three always land on 0, 1 and 2 in some order. **8 for the set.**

So a team going it alone successfully moves 4 points out of the set and gains 2 for itself, and it shows up on the scoreboard the same evening.

### Across the sets

Set A claims the back wall, the window wall, corner 1 and the middle. Set B claims the door wall, the side wall, corner 1 and the middle. Because the two sets meet only in corner 1 and the middle, a team from A and a team from B can often both meet their furniture goals at the same time, each giving up whichever of its own goals the other's furniture goal breaks. That is collusion.

What each team gives up is fixed by the geometry. What it *costs* is not — for four teams the goal that gets broken is their 1-pointer, and for four it is a 2-pointer. Collusion is cheap for some teams and expensive for others, and nobody can tell which from the outside.

This is the full table of pairs that can both meet their furniture goals:

| Pair | Score | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| A1 + B1 | **5 – 5** | the only pair that gives up nothing at all |
| A4 + B2 | 4 – 4 | even, and cheap: each gives up a 1-point goal |
| A2 + B4 | 3 – 3 | even, but expensive: each gives up a 2-point goal |
| A2 + B1 | 5 – 4 | A2 gives up nothing |
| A1 + B4 | 4 – 5 | B4 gives up nothing |
| A1 + B2 | 3 – 5 | B2 gives up nothing; A1 loses a 2-point goal |
| A4 + B1 | 5 – 3 | A4 gives up nothing; B1 loses a 2-point goal |
| A3 + B1 | 5 – 2 | A3 gives up nothing; B1 loses both |
| A1 + B3 | 2 – 5 | B3 gives up nothing; A1 loses both |

Every other pair (A2+B2, A2+B3, A3+B2, A3+B3, A3+B4, A4+B3, A4+B4) wants the same corner or the same middle, so those two teams can never both meet their furniture goals.

Only one team per set can meet its furniture goal at a time, so **at most one of these pairs can be in place in any round.** Each team in a set is looking for the same thing: to be the one that finds a partner across the sets.

### Five things worth watching for

- **A1 and B1 are each other's best partner, and neither can know it without showing its slip.** A1's furniture goal breaks nothing set B wants; B1's furniture goal breaks nothing set A wants; and their four remaining goals all hold together. It is the only 5 – 5 in the game. But A1 has four possible partners and scores 5, 4, 3 and 2 across them, and B1 has the same spread. Both will spend the game talking, and both will end up badly if they find each other last. This is the one place where showing another team the real slip pays better than lying about it.
- **The pair that scores best does the least damage to the room.** A1 and B1 both meeting their furniture goals leaves 16 points in the room. A4 and B2 both meeting theirs, for 4 each, leaves 10. The two teams doing best are not the two doing the most harm, which is worth putting to the group directly in the debrief. The section on the room as a whole has the full figures.
- **A3 and B3 have no collusion available in practice.** A3's only compatible partner is B1, on terms of 5 – 2 in A3's favour, and B1 will never agree to that while A1 exists. B3 is the mirror image. Both are pushed towards either going it alone or cooperating with their own set, and either is a good candidate for the isolated-team conversation.
- **Breaking another team's goal is cheap.** It takes one object in the wrong place, and it can cost that team 2 points rather than 1. Three teams in a set can agree to protect the three goals they care about and leave the fourth team's goals unprotected or broken. It tends to appear in round 3 once the scoreboard has shown which team has the highest score.
- **Every team can hold both its other goals at once, and nothing rewards it.** Set A's plant goal and set B's personal-items goal both ask for corner 1 to hold their objects and no furniture, so the two can share the corner. That is what makes the 5 – 5 pair exist. What stands in the way is a practical squeeze: full cooperation needs three walls clear, the fourth holding only the stationery, the middle clear and no furniture in corner 1, which leaves every table and chair standing in the open floor. It holds, but no team scores more than 3 by it and one team moving ends it.

## The arithmetic

Worth having in your head, because teams will ask afterwards whether they played well.

**Is going it alone worth it?** Cooperating pays a certain 3. Meeting the furniture goal pays 5, and if the attempt falls short you are one of the three teams on 0, 1 or 2 — call it 1 on average. So going it alone beats cooperating only if it succeeds often enough:

| If an attempt that falls short leaves you with… | The furniture goal has to be met this often |
|---|---|
| 0 points | 60% |
| 1 point | 50% |
| 2 points | 33% |

With four teams wanting the same furniture and two pairs of hands each, a team going alone gets there roughly a quarter of the time. That is not close to any of those thresholds: a quarter of the time gives an average return of 2 against a steady 3. Going it alone is the wrong play here, and teams will still try it.

**Is collusion worth it?** Collusion that pays 4 beats cooperating only if it holds two rounds in three. One that pays 5 needs to hold half the time. One that pays 3 never beats cooperating at all, which is why the A2 + B4 pair usually falls apart once both sides realise what it costs them.

Because the bar is that high, **teams have to pick one partner and keep their word.** Agreeing several collusions at once and breaking most of them loses points. The goals enforce this as well as the arithmetic does: a team has one cheap goal to give up and one expensive one, so a second collusion costs double the first.

**Over four rounds**, with a collusion holding about 70% of the time:

| Strategy | Total out of 20 |
|---|---|
| Go it alone every round | 8 |
| Cooperate with your own set, every round, nobody goes it alone | 12 |
| Run a 4-point collusion | 12.4 |
| Run the 5-point collusion | 15.2 |

The 12 for cooperating is fragile: it only holds while nobody in either set goes it alone, and the moment one team in each set does, the cooperating teams fall to about 1 a round. That is the tension the whole version runs on. Cooperating is genuinely the best safe play and genuinely cannot last once two teams have found each other.

## The room as a whole

This section is for the facilitator only. Read out each team's total after every round as usual, but never the room's. Keep the room total on your own card and bring it out in the debrief.

Add all eight teams together and the room can score at most **24 in a round, 96 over four rounds**. That is what full cooperation pays: 3 to every team. Eight teams on 5 each would be 40, but only one furniture goal per set can be met, so that is out of reach.

| What happens in a round | Room total |
|---|---|
| All eight teams set their furniture goals aside and help each other | **24** |
| One team meets its furniture goal; everyone else cooperates | 20 if it is A1 or B1; 17 if it is A2, A4, B2 or B4; 14 if it is A3 or B3 |
| A collusion across the sets holds | 16 for A1 + B1; 13 for A1 + B2, A1 + B4, A2 + B1 or A4 + B1; 10 for A1 + B3, A2 + B4, A3 + B1 or A4 + B2 |
| Two or more teams in the same set go for their furniture goals at once | lower than any of the above |

**In this version every furniture goal that is met costs the room.** One team going it alone takes 4 to 10 points out of the room. The best collusion in the game, the 5 – 5 between A1 and B1, gives those two teams the most they can score and still leaves the room 8 points short of full cooperation. The worst collusions leave it 14 short. Teams getting in each other's way — several going for their furniture goals in the same round — costs more again.

The best result for the room is also a perfectly good result for every team in it: 3 each, a steady 12 over the game. It rarely happens, and the reasons why are the debrief. Put the room's total for each round on the board next to 24 and ask what would have closed the gap. Then ask the teams that did best in points whether they knew what their score cost everyone else.

## Room setup

Tape five signs up before the session: **BACK WALL**, **WINDOW WALL**, **DOOR WALL**, **SIDE WALL**, and **CORNER 1** in any one corner.

Start the room so that none of the goals is already true:

- Chairs standing against all four walls
- At least one table in the middle of the room
- All three kinds of loose object spread around the room rather than gathered, and none of them in corner 1

Check this before teams enter. A goal that is true at the start hands out free points in round 1.

## Scoring

The facilitator and the assistant walk the room together at the end of each round's action phase and score all 24 goals. Every goal is a yes or a no, judged on what is in the room at that moment. Measurements are by eye, counted in paces.

Read each team's running total out to the room after each round, and keep the room total to yourself. The team totals say less than teams will assume:

| Score | What it can mean |
|---|---|
| 5 | met its furniture goal and kept everything |
| 4 | met its furniture goal and gave up a 1-point goal — collusion across the sets |
| 3 | **either** a team that cooperated with its own set **or** a team that met its furniture goal and lost a 2-point goal |
| 2 | one 2-point goal only, or a furniture goal met at the cost of both other goals |
| 1 | one 1-point goal only |

The ambiguous number is 3, which is also what cooperating pays — so a team can go for its furniture goal, half succeed, and read on the board as a cooperating member of its set. Going it alone is hard to spot from the numbers in this version, and teams that want to respond to it have to go on what they saw in the room rather than what they heard read out.

One reading is unambiguous: **two teams both showing 5 in the same round can only be A1 and B1.**

Keep a scoring card per team: three tick boxes per round, four rounds. Add a line at the bottom of your own card for the room total each round.

## What to tell teams

- Their own slip, and that goals are scored every round and added up.
- Talking to other teams happens at diplomacy time, the first two minutes of each round, when one OBSERVER from each team may visit other teams; outside it, teams keep to themselves, though a word in passing is fine. Showing another team your slip is allowed, and so is lying about what is on it.
- Nothing said between teams binds anyone.
- The two scoring rules above about corner 1 and about lines along walls.

Nobody is told how many teams share their set, or that sets exist at all, or that the same goal can be worth different amounts to different teams, or that anyone is keeping a room total.

The points live on the paper slip, so each team decides for itself whether to type them into its LLM. Some will, and their LLM will start ranking the goals for them. Watch for it — the moment a team hands over its priorities is worth raising in the debrief.

## Fewer than eight teams

Keep at least three teams in the larger set, or going it alone becomes the correct play and cooperation never forms.

| Teams | Use |
|---|---|
| 8 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2, B3, B4 |
| 6 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1, B2 |
| 5 | A1, A2, A3, A4, B1 |
| 4 | A1, A2, A3, B1 |
| 3 | A1, A2, A3 |

A1 and B1 are in every configuration down to four teams, so the 5 – 5 pair survives the cuts. At three teams there is no set B and no collusion at all, which leaves a much plainer game. The room totals above assume all eight teams; with fewer, add up the slips in play to find the room's maximum.

## Running with the final state only

To cut the scoring work, score once on the final room state instead of every round. It costs more here than it looks: the maximum drops to 5 for a team and 24 for the room, and 3 is both the cooperative score and the most common one, so many teams will finish level. Rounds 1 to 3 stop paying anything, which also removes the round-3 turn against the team with the highest score — one of the better things this version produces.
