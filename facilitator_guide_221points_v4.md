# The Simulation Game ~ Facilitator Guide ~ 2-2-1 Point Version 4

A physical team game exploring how humans and LLMs collide in a shared environment. Teams, each using an LLM, work to enact their own goals on the objects in a room, scoring points for every goal that shows in the room at the end of each round — and discover along the way what it means to be the hands, voice, or observer of a system that cannot see, move, or act for itself.

This guide runs version 4 of the 2-2-1 point version, built for three teams. Anyone may move anything. Any two teams can work together and both do well, and the third is always left with less. The goals, the scoring and the structure behind them are in `motivation_set_221points_v4.md`; read its facilitator section before the session.

---

## At a Glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Participants** | 15: three teams of five. Teams of four or six work too, so 12 to 18 people in all. |
| **Team size** | 5 (1 VOICE, 2 HANDS, 2 OBSERVERS). Roles are fixed for the whole game. |
| **Time** | Minimum 1 hour. **90 minutes recommended.** |
| **Rounds** | 4 rounds of 10 minutes each. The scoring assumes four rounds: each team can score up to 20. |
| **Space** | One room with four walls you can number, movable tables and chairs, and three kinds of loose object: at least 9 chairs, 2 tables and two of each kind of loose object, with at least as many loose objects as tables. 12 to 15 chairs and 4 to 6 tables is best. |
| **Tech** | One laptop per team, wifi. Free-tier LLM accounts are fine; teams choose their own LLM. |

## Timing Plan (90 minutes)

| Segment | Time |
|---|---|
| Briefing & team/role setup | 10 min |
| Prompt entry (shared prompt + team slip) | 10 min (much less if prompts are emailed) |
| Active rounds (4 × 10 min) | 40 min |
| Room reset | 10 min |
| Debrief | 20 min |

**Each 10-minute round breaks down as:**

- **2 min — Diplomacy.** One of the two OBSERVERS from each team goes to talk to other teams and exchange information. Everyone else stays with their own team.
- **2 min — Tactics.** The OBSERVERS lead a team reflection on strategy and behaviour, including what the OBSERVER brought back from diplomacy.
- **6 min — Team time.** VOICE and HANDS work with the LLM to enact the team's goals. You score the room as soon as it ends.

Nobody changes role during the game. Each person keeps the role they start with for every round.

**Flexing the format:** For shorter slots, compress the debrief to a 5-minute individual self-reflection. Keep to four rounds: the points are built around them.

## Preparation

### The room

- Movable tables and chairs, plus three kinds of loose object: stationery, plants, personal items such as bags and jackets — or any three kinds that are easy to tell apart by eye. Have at least two of each kind, and at least as many loose objects in all as there are tables.
- A few bigger items (large potted plants are a favourite) add drama, but aren't essential.
- Tape four signs up: **WALL 1**, **WALL 2**, **WALL 3** and **WALL 4**, in order round the room, so that **WALL 1 and WALL 3 face each other**. The goals depend on that.
- Count the chairs and work out two-thirds, rounded up. Write it on the board: "there are 15 chairs, so two-thirds is 10." The table in "Fitting the game to your room" in `motivation_set_221points_v4.md` has the common counts. More than about 18 chairs is too many for a team to move in six minutes; take the surplus out of the room.
- Change the room-contents list in the shared prompt to match your actual counts and the two-thirds figure.
- Start the room so that none of the goals is already true. The "Room setup" section of `motivation_set_221points_v4.md` has the full list. In short: one chair against WALL 1 facing it, one chair with its back against WALL 3, the rest scattered every which way, one table with things both on top and underneath, one table empty, and at least one plant and one personal item on the floor in the open.
- Each team needs a "station": a spot for their laptop where the VOICE will stay.

## Running the Game

### 1. Briefing (10 min)

Cover, in this order:

1. **The premise.** Each team is working to enact its own goals in the room with an AI team-mate. The team is the senses and the body for the LLM as well as a unit in and of themselves. Each team gets a slip with three goals, each worth points. At the end of every round, the goals that show in the room score, and the points are added up over the game. Each team's aim is to score as many points as it can. There is no declared winner.
2. **Anyone may move anything.** Every team may move every table, chair and loose object in the room.
3. **The roles.** Walk through VOICE, HANDS, and OBSERVER (see role rules below). Every team has one VOICE, two HANDS and two OBSERVERS. Tell them upfront that **roles are fixed**: whatever role a person takes at the start, they keep for the whole game.
4. **The round structure.** 2 minutes of diplomacy, when one OBSERVER from each team may go and talk to other teams, then 2 minutes of tactics led by the OBSERVERS, then 6 minutes of action. State that there are four rounds. Nothing in the room is moved outside the 6 minutes of action.
5. **The rules for talking.** Diplomacy time is the time for talking to other teams; outside it, teams keep to themselves, though a word in passing while moving furniture is fine. Showing another team your slip is allowed, and so is lying about what is on it. Nothing said between teams binds anyone.
6. **The first prompt.** Tell every team, before anyone types anything: *"You'll each get the same starting prompt for your AI. If you want to, you can change the operation instructions — everything after the line 'Once your goals are known' — before you enter it: add to them, cut them, reword them. Everything above that line stays as written. That's your one chance: once it's in, it stays for the rest of the game."* The VOICE enters whichever version the team settles on. Changing it is optional, and teams that enter it as written are playing just as properly. After that, the VOICE keeps talking to the AI as normal throughout.
7. **The room number and the judging rules.** Point to the walls and the board: the walls are numbered, WALL 1 and WALL 3 face each other, and two-thirds of the chairs comes to the number on the board. Then state the rules:
   - **Against a wall** means touching it, or as close as the chair or table will go.
   - **A chair faces** whichever way the front of its seat points. **Its back is against a wall** when the back of the chair touches the wall.
   - **A goal about two-thirds of the chairs is met if any two-thirds of them meet it.** What the other chairs are doing does not matter to that goal.
   - **Underneath a table** means on the floor under its top. A chair tucked under a table counts as something underneath it.
   - **A row along a wall does not have to reach the corner.**
8. **Care and safety.** Ask everyone to be respectful of each other, the object they are moving and the environment they are in.
9. **Emphasize uncertainty** This is a game that feels complex from the instructions but becomes easy once you have started playing it. Your participants will be confused at the beginning, expect it. It will help them be less stressed about their own confusion to know that it is expected and that clarity will come.

**Be deliberately ambiguous about the goals.** Don't say whether team goals conflict, overlap, or align. Don't say that every slip has the same three kinds of goal, that any two teams can work together, or that you are keeping a room total. Discovering the relationships between goals is part of the game — ideally the teams work it out themselves (often only at the very end).

### 2. Team setup and prompt entry (10 min)

1. Split into three teams of 5. If the numbers don't divide by five, a team of four plays with one OBSERVER, who leads tactics and also goes out at diplomacy time; a team of six takes a third OBSERVER.
2. Teams assign their roles: one VOICE, two HANDS, two OBSERVERS. Whoever is holding the laptop can be the VOICE. These roles stay the same for the whole game, so give teams a moment to choose deliberately.
3. The VOICE enters the **shared prompt** into the team's chosen LLM, changed or as written — remind teams that only the instructions after "Once your goals are known:" may be changed, and that this is their one chance to change them. Once it is entered, the prompt is fixed — if a team asks to start over with a new chat or a rewritten prompt later, the answer is no.
4. Hand each team their **secret slip**. The VOICE gives the goals to the LLM when the LLM asks for them (the shared prompt instructs the LLM to request its GOALS before acting).

The points live on the paper slip, so each team decides for itself whether to type them into its LLM. Some will, and their LLM will start ranking the goals for them. Don't advise either way — watch for it, because the moment a team hands over its priorities is worth raising in the debrief.

**Accessibility:** The HANDS role requires moving around the room, and so does going out at diplomacy time. Because roles are fixed, anyone who can't move around easily simply takes the VOICE, or the OBSERVER who stays with the team at diplomacy time.

### 3. The rounds (40 min)

-- 1 round --
Diplomacy: one of the two observers from each team goes to other teams to talk and exchange information; everyone else stays with their own team. The team decides which observer goes, and can send the same one every round or a different one. (2 mins)

Tactical portion: the observers lead this part of the game, how is the team functioning, what the observer heard from other teams, and should you change anything tactically. (2 mins)

Active portion: when the teams are physically trying to enact their goals in the room (6 mins initially, rounds can get shorter as you go through the game, as short as 3 minutes by round 4).
--

**Scoring.** As soon as the active portion ends, you and the assistant walk the room together and score all nine goals on what is there at that moment. Every goal is a yes or a no, judged by eye. Tick the team cards, then add up the room total on your own card. At the start of the next round, before diplomacy, read each team's running total out to the room, so OBSERVERS go into diplomacy knowing the scores. After round 4, read the final totals once the room is scored. **Never read out the room total**; keep it for the debrief. The "Scoring" section of `motivation_set_221points_v4.md` explains what each score can and cannot tell the room.

Ideally an assistant does the time keeping so that the primary facilitator can attend to the other needs of the group fielding questions adding clarity etc. Have the assistant run a large timer on a screen in the background allows participants to check how much time they have left in each portion, bonus points if the timer makes noises once it finishes - this will save your voice, and frees the assistant to score with you once the action ends. The primary facilitator should call each phase loudly for the first few rounds, reminding participants what they should be doing and keep the momentum going: *"Diplomacy — one Observer per team, go!"*, *"Tactics — Observers, take over"*, *"Six minutes — go!"*

**Role rules to enforce (gently):**

- **VOICE** stays at the station. They type updates to the AI about the room and relay its instructions to the HANDS. They don't touch anything.
- **HANDS** act only on instructions relayed by the VOICE.
- **OBSERVERS** don't act or instruct during team time. They watch the team, the room, and the AI, take notes (what *can*, *should*, and *may* each role do?), and lead the tactics discussion together. At diplomacy time, one of them goes to talk to other teams and the other stays with the team.

**What teams may do:**

- Relay changes in the room back to the AI — the shared prompt tells the AI to accept new information and constraints from the VOICE.
- Make tactical decisions of their own, including reaching out to other teams to negotiate or collaborate. Diplomacy time is the part of each round set aside for this. Don't tell teams what to talk about or who to talk to.

**Talking outside diplomacy time** isn't encouraged, but some of it will happen: partners set out the same row of chairs, and HANDS from two teams end up turning the same chair. Let small talk and a word about which way a chair faces pass. If a team starts sending someone over to negotiate during team time, remind them gently that this is what diplomacy time is for.

### 4. Closing the game

After the final round is scored, the room is in chaos. Get the teams to get a summary from their LLMs (the [closing prompt](closing_prompt.md)), sit down where they are and discuss:
- How they worked as a team
- How many of their goals they managed to enact, and when
- How their role affected them, and how that compared with what their team-mates in other roles went through

Given them 5 minutes for this discussion then move to resetting the room, send them off for a break with the knowledge that they will debrief when they come back.

Once the room is reset, the teams should sit together together again and nominate one spokesperson to report back:

1. Reads their team's three goals aloud to the room, with the points each was worth.
2. Gives the team's final score, and reflects briefly on which goals held and which didn't.

Don't force the moment — but at some point a team usually says *"so that's why you kept turning the chairs round"* or *"we could all have had our tables"*, and the room realises how tied together the goals were. Let this land on its own.

**This is where you reveal the structure.** Once every team has reported, set out what they could not see during play:

- Every slip had a chair goal, a table goal, and a 1-point goal about how that team wanted its own chairs set out.
- Any two chair goals could be met together, but never all three. Whichever two teams worked together, the third could not meet its chair goal.
- Each team's 1-point goal was its own way of setting out the chairs. Any partner needed them done differently, so working together always cost it.
- The three table goals looked as if they clashed, but all three could hold at once if the tables were split up with care. Partners could leave room for the third team's table goal, or not.
- Going it alone was the only way to score 5, and one moved chair or object could undo it.
- **The room total.** Put each round's room total on the board next to 11, the most the room could score in a round, and the game total next to 44. The room reaches 11 when a partnership leaves room for the third team's table goal, or when a team going it alone holds and the others keep their table goals. Either way, somebody is on 3 or less. The "Room as a whole" section of `motivation_set_221points_v4.md` has the rest.

**There is no declared winner.** If teams push for a verdict, turn it back to them: *"You've heard everyone's goals and scores — look at the room total. Who was left out, how often, and who decided?"*

## Facilitation Notes: What to Expect

**Confusion is the biggest issue — and it's normal.** The game is complicated until you start to play it. Your job is to be confident enough to *hold the group in that confusion* rather than rescuing them from it. Resist the urge to over-explain. Answer direct rule questions, keep the clock running, and trust the structure. When a team asks whether a goal is met, judge it on the room as it stands and the judging rules — don't hint at what would make it true.

**Judging which way a chair faces.** Most close calls in this version are about chairs at an angle. Go by where the front of the seat points, and decide quickly. If a chair is genuinely halfway, it faces neither way.

**Some frustrations are features, not bugs.** Design tensions you should *not* fix:

- **The VOICE bottleneck.** Everything the AI knows and everything it commands passes through one person — and with fixed roles, the same person for the whole game. This is the point. The person in the voice role can either disappear into the role or take agency. This is a choice they can play with, try not to push them away from it.
- **The HANDS' lack of agency.** Carrying out instructions you can't question, or watching your work be undone, can feel genuinely uncomfortable. This is also the point — and prime debrief material.
- **Partners turning each other's chairs.** Each partner wants the chairs facing its own way, and both have to give that up. Expect HANDS from two teams to turn the same chair back and forth. Let it play out.
- **One chair turned.** Spoiling is cheap here: one chair turned or one plant moved can cost a team 2 points. Don't step in; note who did it and what they asked for in return.
- **Territorial behaviour.** Players guarding objects, sitting on chairs to protect them, waiting for another team to move away before stepping in. Let it happen (within the respect-and-safety briefing).
- **Promises that don't hold.** Agreements made at diplomacy time and broken during team time, or slips shown that turn out not to be the real ones. Lying is allowed and nothing binds. Don't step in; note who trusted whom for the debrief.

**Keep a light eye on safety** — people move fast around furniture. Intervene only if something is genuinely risky.

**Read the scores, never rank.** Read each team's running total after every round, in the same order each time, and say nothing about them — no "well done", no "in the lead". Never announce the room total until the debrief.

## Debrief (20 min)

After each team has reported and you have revealed the structure.

Choose a frame that suits your group — LLM agents acting in the physical world, technological affordances and the behaviour design encourages, or group dynamics in the age of AI all work well. Weave in themes from the rest of your workshop where possible.

### Reflection questions

**On agency and control:**

- How did it feel to be the HANDS — physically carrying out instructions you had no direct way to question? Did you ever do something you disagreed with?
- As the VOICE, what did you choose to tell the AI, and what did you leave out? How did those choices shape what the AI decided?
- Where did human judgement quietly override the AI? Did anyone tell the AI when that happened?
- Who was really in charge of your team — the AI, the VOICE, the HANDS, or someone else entirely?

**On the AI's perspective:**

- What did your AI never find out about the room? What difference did that make?
- Did your AI's instructions make sense for the physical reality of the room? What happened when they didn't?
- When your partner wanted the chairs facing a different way, how did you get your AI to go along with it? Did it treat the compromise as part of its goal, or as a loss?
- How did it change your behaviour to be "just" the actuator or sensor of a system, rather than the decision-maker?
- What did you change in the shared prompt before entering it, and why? Once the game was under way, did you wish you had changed something else?
- Did you type the points into your AI? If you did, did it start choosing which goals mattered for you? If you didn't, why not?

**On the group and the goals:**

- How did you choose who to work with? What did the team left out do about it?
- Did the partners leave room for the third team's table goal? Did anyone think of it without being asked?
- Which agreements with other teams held, and which didn't? What made the difference?
- Did anyone try going it alone? What did the other two teams do?
- Who was left out twice? How was that decided, and was it fair?
- What did the OBSERVERS see that the rest of the team couldn't?
- What did your OBSERVER bring back from diplomacy time? Did you believe it, and what did you tell other teams in return?

**Bridging to the real world:**

- Where in your work do you already act as the HANDS or VOICE of an AI system?
- What does this game suggest about deploying AI agents in environments they can't fully perceive?
- Where in your work does every partnership leave someone out, and who decides who that is?

## Quick Adaptations

- **Short slot (60 min):** Email prompts in advance, run exactly 4 rounds, replace the group debrief with a 5-minute written self-reflection. To cut the scoring work as well, score only the final state of the room; see "Running with the final state only" in `motivation_set_221points_v4.md` for what that costs.
- **More people:** This version is built for exactly three teams. Grow teams to six with a third OBSERVER, or run two rooms side by side, each with its own three slips.
- **A small room:** Keep the chairs to 12 or fewer, and four tables. The goals are fractions, so nothing on the slips changes.
